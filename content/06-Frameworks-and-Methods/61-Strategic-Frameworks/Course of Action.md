> [!bluf]
> A **course of action (COA)** is one possible way to accomplish the mission. US doctrine makes planners develop **several distinct COAs**, test each against the enemy in a **wargame**, compare them against agreed **criteria**, and let the commander choose one, which becomes the plan. This sits at the heart of both the Army's **Military Decision-Making Process (MDMP)** and the **Joint Planning Process (JPP)**. Each COA must pass a screen: it must be **feasible, acceptable, suitable, distinguishable and complete**. The method guards against falling in love with the first idea, but it is slow; under time pressure, experienced commanders tend to decide by recognition instead.

## Purpose

The COA method turns a mission into a **choice between real alternatives**. It rests on a simple insight: the first idea is rarely the best, and a plan tested only against itself looks better than it is. So the process forces planners to:

- produce **more than one** way of doing the job,
- make them **genuinely different**,
- **fight** each one in the mind against a thinking enemy, and
- compare them **openly**, so the commander's choice is informed and traceable.

## Components / Steps

### Where COAs sit in the process

| Step | Army MDMP [@usarmy2024planning] | Joint Planning Process [@jcs2020joint] |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Receipt of mission | Planning initiation |
| 2 | Mission analysis | Mission analysis |
| 3 | **COA development** | **COA development** |
| 4 | **COA analysis (war game)** | **COA analysis and wargaming** |
| 5 | **COA comparison** | **COA comparison** |
| 6 | **COA approval** | **COA approval** |
| 7 | Orders production, dissemination and transition | Plan or order development |

Steps 3–6 are the COA core. Mission analysis before them produces what they need: the specified and implied tasks, constraints, facts and assumptions, the enemy picture (often structured with [[PMESII]]), the **commander's intent** and planning guidance.

### 1. Develop

A COA answers **who, what, when, where, why and how** at the level of the unit's subordinate elements. It is written as a short **COA statement** and a **sketch**. In the Army, developing a COA typically runs through: assess relative combat power, generate options, array forces, develop a broad concept, assign headquarters, and prepare the statement and sketch.

Each COA must pass the **screening criteria** [@usarmy2024planning; @jcs2020joint]:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| **Feasible** | Can it be done with the forces, time and resources available? |
| **Acceptable** | Is the expected cost (casualties, time, political risk) worth the gain? |
| **Suitable** (joint: *adequate*) | Does it accomplish the mission within the commander's intent and guidance? |
| **Distinguishable** | Is it clearly different from the other COAs (not just a variant in timing or task organisation)? |
| **Complete** | Does it cover the whole operation, including follow-on and sustainment? |

### 2. Analyse (wargame)

Each COA is played through **action – reaction – counteraction** against the most likely and the most dangerous **enemy COAs**. The wargame reveals what the COA needs in timing, coordination and resources, and where the **decision points** lie. Common methods are the *belt* (phase lines across the area), the *avenue-in-depth* (one route at a time) and the *box* (one critical area). The wargame is also where the COA is refined: gaps found here are fixed before comparison.

### 3. Compare

The staff compares COAs against **evaluation criteria** set in advance, often derived from the commander's guidance and the principles of war (e.g. simplicity, flexibility, risk, speed, casualties). A **decision matrix** with weighted criteria is the typical tool. Its numbers support the recommendation; they do not replace judgement.

### 4. Approve

The staff briefs the comparison and a recommendation. The commander chooses, modifies or combines COAs, and issues refined intent and guidance for writing the plan or order.

### Swiss equivalent

The Swiss command process in *FSO 17* follows the same logic under different names [@schweizerarmee2017fso]. It has five command activities: problem recognition, assessment of the situation (*Beurteilung der Lage*), decision (*Entschlussfassung*), plan development and issuing orders. Immediate measures and time planning run alongside them. During the assessment, the staff develops **variants** (*Varianten*), checks them and sets them against each other. The commander's **decision** (*Entschluss*) states how they intend to accomplish the mission.

The Swiss checks for a variant match the US screen almost one to one:

| FSO 17 | Question | US equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| *Angemessenheit* | Does it accomplish the mission? | suitable / adequate |
| *Exklusivität* | Does it differ from the other variants? | distinguishable |
| *Machbarkeit* | Are the means sufficient? | feasible |
| *Tragbarkeit* | Are the risks bearable? | acceptable |
| *Vollständigkeit* | Does it say when, who, what, where, how? | complete |

## Worked Example

**Mission (simplified):** a battalion is to secure a bridge 20 km ahead so a brigade can cross in 36 hours. An enemy reconnaissance company is near the bridge; enemy reserves are 30 km away.

| | **COA 1: Direct thrust** | **COA 2: Envelopment** | **COA 3: Air-mobile seizure** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concept | Main body advances on the main road, seizes the bridge by force | Fix the enemy frontally with one company; main body crosses a ford 8 km north and takes the bridge from the flank | Light infantry is flown in to seize the bridge by surprise; main body links up by road |
| Feasible? | yes | yes, if the ford holds vehicles | only if helicopters are available and air defence is suppressed |
| Distinguishable? | yes: frontal | yes: indirect | yes: vertical |
| Strength | simple; fastest to mass | hits weakness; less risk to the bridge | fastest seizure; surprise |
| Risk | enemy blows the bridge while we fight toward it | slower; relies on the ford | the air-mobile force is isolated if the link-up is delayed |

The wargame against the **most dangerous enemy COA** (the reserve arrives early and counter-attacks) is what really separates them. COA 3 looks best on speed but fails if the link-up is delayed; COA 2 costs time but keeps the force together. The comparison makes that trade-off **visible** so the commander can decide on it deliberately, rather than discover it during execution. *(Invented example.)*

Note how the COAs map onto [[Attrition vs Manoeuvre Warfare|attrition and manoeuvre]]: COA 1 is strength against strength, COA 2 and 3 aim strength at weakness.

## Strengths & Pitfalls

**Strengths**

- **Prevents premature closure.** Forcing several distinct options counters the tendency to stop at the first plausible idea.
- **Tests the plan against a thinking enemy** before execution, not during it.
- **Traceable decisions.** Criteria and comparison make the choice explainable to superiors, subordinates and later reviewers.
- **Produces the details** (decision points, synchronisation, resource needs) that the order needs anyway.

**Pitfalls**

- **The "favourite plus two strawmen".** The staff already prefers one COA and builds weak alternatives to make it look good. The *distinguishable* criterion exists against this.
- **Spreadsheet decisions.** A weighted matrix gives an air of objectivity to criteria and weights that were chosen subjectively.
- **Time.** The full process can take days. Doctrine allows an abbreviated version, but staffs often run out of time for the wargame, the step that matters most.
- **The enemy's COA is also a guess.** A wargame is only as good as the picture of the enemy it is played against.

> [!counter] Critique
> - **Recognition-primed decision making.** Klein's field studies of experienced decision-makers (fire commanders, military officers) found they rarely compare options. They **recognise** the situation as typical, take the first workable option, and test it by **mental simulation**; only if it fails do they consider another [@klein1998sources]. On this view, the COA method describes how novices should decide, or how staffs should justify, more than how experts decide under time pressure.
> - **Speed and tempo.** A process built for thoroughness can make a force slow to adapt. In [[OODA Loop|Boyd's]] terms, an elaborate *Decide* phase risks acting on a situation that has already changed.
> - **Planning versus design.** Critics argue that the MDMP is good at solving well-defined problems but poor at *framing* ill-defined ones, which is why US doctrine added a separate "design" or conceptual planning step before it.

## Self-Test: COA

> [!qard]- 1. What are the COA steps in the MDMP, and what comes before them?
> Development, analysis (wargame), comparison, approval. Before them: receipt of mission and mission analysis (tasks, constraints, enemy picture, commander's intent and guidance).

> [!qard]- 2. Name the five screening criteria and explain *distinguishable*.
> Feasible, acceptable, suitable (joint: adequate), distinguishable, complete. *Distinguishable* means each COA must differ significantly from the others (e.g. in scheme of manoeuvre or main effort), not just in details, so the commander has a real choice.

> [!qard]- 3. Why wargame each COA?
> To test it against a reacting enemy (most likely and most dangerous COA), find decision points, timing and resource needs, and fix weaknesses before comparison and execution.

> [!qard]- 4. What is Klein's critique, and when is it most relevant?
> Experts decide by recognising the situation and simulating one option mentally, not by comparing several. It matters most under time pressure and for experienced commanders; the COA method remains valuable for staffs, novices, novel problems and decisions that must be justified.

> [!qard]- 5. Apply: a staff presents three COAs, its favourite and two that differ from it only in timing. Which criterion fails, and why does it matter?
> *Distinguishable*. Without real alternatives the comparison is a formality and the commander has nothing to choose from. This is the "favourite plus two strawmen" pitfall.

> [!qard]- 6. Compare: when does a formal COA comparison fit, and when does a recognition-primed decision?
> Comparison fits a staff with time, a new kind of problem and a decision that must be traceable. A recognition-primed decision fits an experienced leader under time pressure in a familiar situation: take the first workable option and test it in the mind.
