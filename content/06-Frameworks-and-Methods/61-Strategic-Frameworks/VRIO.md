> [!bluf]
> VRIO tests whether a resource or capability gives a **lasting advantage** over competitors. It asks four questions in order:
> - **Value:** does it let us exploit an opportunity or neutralise a threat?
> - **Rarity:** do only we, or very few others, have it?
> - **Imitability:** is it hard, costly or slow for others to copy?
> - **Organisation:** are we set up to actually use it?
>
> Each "yes" moves the result up one level, from *competitive disadvantage* to *sustained competitive advantage*. The framework comes from the **resource-based view** of the firm (Barney). Hofstetter, Borioli and Flemming carry it over to armed forces as **CRIO** (*combat value* instead of value) to explain when a military innovation can break a stalemate, and when the advantage fades.

## Purpose

Most strategy tools look **outward**, at the market or the enemy. VRIO looks **inward**: *what do we have that others don't, and can they get it?* It is the working tool of the **resource-based view (RBV)**, which explains lasting differences in performance by what an organisation *is* (its resources and capabilities), not only by where it stands in the market [@wernerfelt1984resourcebased; @barney1991firm].

Barney's argument rests on two assumptions. First, resources are **heterogeneous**: organisations own different bundles of them. Second, resources are **immobile**: they cannot be bought or moved freely between organisations. If both held false, every advantage would be copied or bought away quickly. VRIO asks whether a given resource is one of those that *stay* different [@barney1991firm].

**Resources** are what you have (money, equipment, land, a patent, information). **Capabilities** are what you can do with them (train, integrate, command, learn). In practice, the capabilities are usually where lasting advantage sits, because they are hard to see and hard to copy.

## Components / Steps

![[vrio-ladder.svg]]

Ask the four questions **in order**. A "no" stops the ladder and gives the result on that rung [@barney1995looking].

1. **Value (V)**

   Does the resource let the organisation exploit an opportunity or neutralise a threat? If not, it is a **competitive disadvantage**: it costs something and gives nothing. Value is always **relative to the environment**: a capability that was valuable in one war can become worthless in the next.

1. **Rarity (R)**

   Is it controlled by only one or a few competitors? A valuable but common resource gives **competitive parity**: you need it to stay in the game, but it does not set you apart.

1. **Imitability (I)**

   Is it costly or slow for others to copy or substitute? If not, a valuable and rare resource gives only a **temporary competitive advantage**: it lasts until others catch up. Barney names three reasons copying is hard [@barney1991firm]:
   - **Unique history** (*path dependence*): it was built over time, under conditions that cannot be repeated.
   - **Causal ambiguity:** not even the owner fully understands *why* it works, so others cannot see what to copy.
   - **Social complexity:** it lives in relationships, culture and trust, not in a single object.

1. **Organisation (O)**

   Are structures, processes, incentives and culture in place to use the resource? If not, the advantage is **unexploited**. This question works like an adjustment factor: it does not create advantage itself, but it decides whether the other three are turned into results.

| V | R | I | O | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| no | | | | competitive **disadvantage** |
| yes | no | | | competitive **parity** |
| yes | yes | no | | **temporary** competitive advantage |
| yes | yes | yes | no | **unexploited** competitive advantage |
| yes | yes | yes | yes | **sustained** competitive advantage |

The framework was first called **VRIN** (*non-substitutable* instead of *organisation*) [@barney1991firm]. Barney added the organisation question and the VRIO label in the mid-1990s [@barney1995looking].

## Military adaptation: CRIO

Hofstetter, Borioli and Flemming (2024) use VRIO to explain how armed forces get out of a stalemate. They keep R, I and O, and replace *value* with **combat value (C)**: does the capability let the force exploit opportunities or neutralise enemy threats? They suggest sub-factors for each question [@hofstetter2024manoeuvre]:

| Factor | Example sub-factors |
|---|---|
| **Combat value** | command and control, networking; air defence; protected ground mobility |
| **Rarity** | scarce resources; rare knowledge; information the enemy does not have |
| **Inimitability** | expensive; time-consuming; complex |
| **Organisation** | doctrinal, cultural and procedural support |

Their **Thesis III** is VRIO applied to innovation: a military innovation that only adds combat value and is rare gives a **temporary** advantage. It lasts only if it is also **hard to imitate** and **supported by the organisation** (doctrine, culture, processes). This links VRIO to their argument about [[Attrition vs Manoeuvre Warfare|attrition and manoeuvre]]: an innovation is what lets a side move from frozen attrition back to manoeuvre, but the window closes as soon as the enemy copies it.

## Worked Example

**Question:** Do cheap reconnaissance and strike drones give a lasting advantage in a war like Russia–Ukraine since 2022?

| Question | Assessment | Result so far |
|---|---|---|
| **Combat value** | Yes. Cheap drones find and hit targets far behind the front and make massed movement very costly. | at least parity |
| **Rarity** | Only at the start. Within months, both sides fielded them in large numbers. | parity |
| **Inimitability** | No. Commercial parts, open designs and fast workshop production make them easy to copy. | stops at parity |
| **Organisation** | Differs between units: fast feedback loops from the front to workshops, and doctrine that integrates drones, matter more than the drone itself. | the *organisational capability* may pass where the object fails |

The example shows the most useful lesson of VRIO: **the object is rarely the advantage**. Drones as objects reach parity quickly. What can stay rare and hard to copy is the **capability** around them: the adaptation cycle, the training system, the link between users and producers. That is *social complexity* and *causal ambiguity* in Barney's sense. It also matches [[OODA Loop|Boyd's]] point that the lasting edge is faster *relative adaptation*, not a single tool. *(My own application; the facts are broad and uncontested, but the verdict on organisation is a judgement.)*

## Strengths & Pitfalls

**Strengths**

- **Simple and ordered.** Four questions, each with a clear stop, force an honest answer to *"is this really an advantage?"*
- **Separates having from using.** The organisation question catches a common failure: buying capabilities that doctrine, training and culture cannot absorb.
- **Explains why advantages fade.** Imitability makes time explicit: every advantage has a half-life.

**Pitfalls**

- **Hindsight.** It is easy to call something valuable and inimitable after it has worked. Before the fact, the questions are much harder to answer.
- **Static snapshot.** VRIO judges a resource at one moment. In war, and in fast markets, the environment that makes a resource valuable keeps changing.
- **Counting objects instead of capabilities.** A list of equipment is not a VRIO analysis. The advantage usually sits in how things are combined.
- **Ignoring the opponent's adaptation.** In business, rivals copy; in war, the enemy also *counters*. A capability can be neutralised without being imitated (e.g. jamming instead of building your own drones).

> [!counter] Critique
> - **Circular?** Priem and Butler argue that the RBV comes close to a tautology: resources that produce advantage are defined as valuable, and valuable resources produce advantage. It then says little that can be tested [@priem2001resourcebased].
> - **Where does value come from?** VRIO takes value as given. It does not explain how to find it; that needs an outward-looking analysis of the environment (e.g. [[PMESII]] or a market analysis).
> - **Transfer to war.** Firms compete for customers under rules; armed forces fight an opponent who tries to destroy their resources and exploit their weaknesses. "Rarity" and "imitability" are only part of it: **counters**, deception and destruction also end an advantage. CRIO is a useful lens, but it is not yet tested against cases.

## Self-Test: VRIO

> [!qard]- 1. Name the four VRIO questions and the result when the ladder stops at each one.
> - **Value** no → competitive disadvantage
> - **Rarity** no → competitive parity
> - **Imitability** no (easy to copy) → temporary advantage
> - **Organisation** no → unexploited advantage
> - All yes → sustained competitive advantage

> [!qard]- 2. Why are capabilities usually more durable sources of advantage than resources?
> Capabilities are built over time (unique history), rest on relationships and culture (social complexity) and are hard even for the owner to explain (causal ambiguity). All three make them costly to copy. Resources such as equipment can often be bought or rebuilt.

> [!qard]- 3. What is CRIO, and what does Thesis III claim?
> The military version of VRIO by Hofstetter, Borioli and Flemming (2024): *combat value* replaces value. Thesis III: an innovation that adds combat value and is rare gives only a temporary advantage; it lasts only if it is also inimitable and supported by the organisation.

> [!qard]- 4. Why is VRIO alone not enough for strategy?
> It looks only inward. It judges what you have, but it cannot say what will be *valuable*; that depends on the environment and the opponent. It needs an outward-looking analysis beside it.

> [!qard]- 5. Apply: run cheap strike drones through CRIO. Where does the ladder stop, and where can a lasting advantage still sit?
> Combat value yes, rarity only at the start, inimitability no: parity. What can stay rare and hard to copy is the capability around the drones: the adaptation cycle, training and doctrine.

> [!qard]- 6. Limit: in war an advantage can end without being imitated. How?
> The enemy can counter it (jamming), deceive it or destroy it. Rarity and imitability come from business competition, where rivals copy. They miss countermeasures.
