> [!bluf]
> Modern political science has two currents:
> - **Empirical-analytical** research wants to **explain**: it looks for cause and effect, starts from hypotheses and tests them against observation. It is the dominant current.
> - **Hermeneutic** research wants to **understand**: it interprets the meaning of texts, speeches and actions by looking at them again and again from different perspectives.
>
> A second distinction cuts across the first: **positive** research says **what is** (and what will be), **normative** research says **what should be**. Both currents can do both. Keep the two distinctions apart: "hermeneutic" does not mean "normative", and "empirical" does not mean "free of values".

## Definition

**Political science** (*Politikwissenschaft*) is the science that deals with one subsystem of society, politics and above all state politics, and with its institutions and actors. This is the lecture's definition. The textbook defines the discipline by its object (political systems, processes and behaviour) and by its analytical tools [@bernauer2025einfuhrung, p. 41].

- It is the **science of politics**, in the same way that economics is the science of the economy. It is not "political science" in the sense of politicised science.
- It is not **civics** (*Staatskunde*). Civics describes institutions. Political science analyses them systematically.

| | Empirical-analytical | Hermeneutic |
|---|---|---|
| **Aim** | Describe and **explain** political reality as objectively as possible | **Understand** the meaning or significance of political phenomena |
| **Key idea** | Cause and effect | Interpretation |
| **Steps** | Question → theoretical explanation → hypotheses → test by observation → generalisation | Question → attempt to understand → take different perspectives → look again and again (hermeneutic circle) → summary and interpretation |
| **Starts with hypotheses?** | Yes | No |
| **Material** | Data on many or few cases | Texts, speeches, the content of what actors say |
| **Structure** | Fixed sequence, see [[Research Design]] | Looser, circular |
| **Example** | Does economic growth lead to democratisation? | Which narratives about democracy shape how parties present themselves in their manifestos? |

In my own words: the first current asks *why does this happen, and does it happen in general?* The second asks *what does this mean to the people involved?*

## Key Points

- **A hypothesis is an expectation stated precisely.** It is what makes the empirical-analytical test possible.
- **Generalisation is an inference to the population with a margin of error.** Results about people are **probabilistic**: tendencies, not laws. People are complex and variables can rarely be manipulated as in a laboratory.
- **Empirical-analytical is not the same as quantitative.** It is often quantitative, but a comparison of two case studies that tests a hypothesis is also empirical-analytical.
- **Objectivity matters in both currents.** Hermeneutic work reaches it differently: by setting out, as factually as possible, how a piece of information depends on its culture and time, and what the communicating actors intended.
- **The hermeneutic circle:** you understand the parts from the whole and the whole from the parts, so you return to the material several times, each time with a better understanding.

### Positive and normative

| | Positive | Normative |
|---|---|---|
| **Says** | What **is** | What **should be** |
| **Means** | "Positive" in the sense of *actually given*, not of *good*. Free of value judgements and independent of the researcher | Analysis that aims at evaluating a political state of affairs |
| **Forward-looking element** | **Prognostic**: predicts (election forecasts) | **Prescriptive**: recommends or prescribes |

The two distinctions cross:

| | Positive | Normative |
|---|---|---|
| **Empirical-analytical** | Are parties and politicians with more money more successful? | The same finding, then judged by a stated criterion: pluralist democracy stresses equal chances, so elections and votes should not be won by money alone |
| **Hermeneutic** | Which narratives about democracy appear in party manifestos? | Is the picture of democracy in these manifestos compatible with the constitution? *(my own example)* |

- **Normative work in empirical-analytical research** starts from a cause-effect relationship and then evaluates it by criteria that are named openly.
- **Forecasts rest on empirical-analytical models.** A good forecast needs a theoretical model that is well validated by data. Predicting state failure relies on data about past failed states. It is harder than predicting elections because the data are much thinner. Election forecasts have their own problem: people can still change their minds.

### Value freedom: where values may enter

Science is not entirely free of value judgements. The question is at which stage they are allowed:

| Stage | Question | Values allowed? |
|---|---|---|
| **Context of discovery** (*Entstehungszusammenhang*) | Which question do I choose? Which problem is relevant? | Yes, unavoidably |
| **Context of justification** (*Begründungszusammenhang*) | How do I test and argue the claim? | **No.** This step must not be shaped by the researcher's own values |
| **Context of use** (*Verwertungszusammenhang*) | How are the results used? | Yes |

The **value-neutrality thesis** demands only that values play no role in the justification. The dispute over it is the *Werturteilsstreit*.

Observation itself is not neutral either: what we see depends on background assumptions (the theory-ladenness of observation, see [[Falsificationism]]).

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Old questions.** Plato, Aristotle, Machiavelli, Hobbes and Kant analysed politics long before the discipline existed.
2. **Modern political science** arose with modern social science in the last 100 to 200 years. Marx and Weber are among its pioneers.
3. **Hermeneutics** comes from the interpretation of religious and legal texts. Courts still work this way when they interpret a statute (see [[Construction of a Legal Case]]).
4. **Explaining versus understanding.** Dilthey made the split a programme: the natural sciences explain, the human sciences understand [@dilthey1883einleitung]. Gadamer later described understanding as a circular movement between the reader's prior understanding and the text [@gadamer1960wahrheit].
5. **Value freedom.** Weber demanded that science separate statements of fact from value judgements, while admitting that values guide the choice of the question [@weber1904objektivitat].
6. **Falsification.** Popper gave the empirical-analytical current its test logic: hypotheses cannot be proven, only refuted [@popper1963conjectures].

## Application

- **Reading a paper:** decide first which current it belongs to and whether it argues positively or normatively. Then judge it by the right standard. A hermeneutic study is not weak because it has no hypothesis test. An empirical-analytical study is not neutral because it has numbers.
- **Strategy and security.** Both currents are in daily use, often without the names:
    - Reading a foreign doctrine, a speech or an order to find out what the author *intends* is hermeneutic work. Intelligence analysis does a great deal of it.
    - Asking whether a type of operation *generally leads* to a certain result, over many cases, is empirical-analytical. See the worked example in [[Bias in Research]].
    - An assessment ("what is the situation?") is positive. A recommendation ("what should be done?") is normative and prescriptive. Mixing the two without saying so is one of the commonest faults of staff papers.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **The split is too clean.** Every measurement needs interpretation: someone must decide what "democracy" means before counting it. And Weber's own programme was to *understand* social action in order to *explain* it [@weber1922wirtschaft, § 1].
> - **Value freedom is contested.** The choice of question, concepts and cases already carries values. Critics hold that research which claims to be neutral only hides its standpoint.
> - **Dominance is not superiority.** The empirical-analytical current is dominant by number of researchers and publications. That is a fact about the discipline, not a proof that it answers the more important questions.
> - **Hermeneutic results are hard to check.** Two readers can arrive at different interpretations, and there is no agreed procedure to decide between them.
> - **Probabilistic findings are easy to over-read.** A tendency across many cases says little about the single case a decision-maker faces.

## Self-Test: Empirical vs Hermeneutic

> [!qard]- 1. Name the typical steps of empirical-analytical research.
> Question → theoretical explanation → hypotheses → test by observation → generalisation.

> [!qard]- 2. What is the aim of hermeneutic research, and how does it proceed?
> To understand the meaning or significance of political phenomena. Question, attempt to understand, taking different perspectives, repeated re-examination (hermeneutic circle), then a summary and interpretation of what was understood.

> [!qard]- 3. Is objectivity less important in hermeneutic studies?
> No. It is reached by presenting and examining, as factually as possible, the cultural and historical dependence of the information and the intentions of the communicating actors.

> [!qard]- 4. Is empirical-analytical research always quantitative?
> Often, but not necessarily. A comparison of two case studies that tests a hypothesis is empirical-analytical too.

> [!qard]- 5. What does "normative" mean in empirical-analytical research? Give an example.
> A cause-effect relationship is the starting point, followed by an evaluation by stated criteria. Example: are well-funded parties more successful? Evaluation: pluralist democracy stresses equal chances, so votes should not be won by money alone.

> [!qard]- 6. Why do forecasts rest mainly on empirical-analytical models?
> A good forecast needs a theoretical model that has been validated by data. Predicting state failure uses data on past failed states. It is harder than forecasting elections because the data are poorer.

> [!qard]- 7. What does "positive" mean here?
> Actually given, describing what is. It does not mean "good".

> [!qard]- 8. Apply: classify (a) an analyst reading a foreign leader's speech to work out his intent, and (b) a study testing across 60 cases whether sanctions change regime behaviour.
> (a) is hermeneutic: understanding meaning and intent. (b) is empirical-analytical: a cause-effect hypothesis tested against many cases. Both are positive as long as they say what *is*.

> [!qard]- 9. Judge: a staff paper moves from "the situation is X" to "so we must do Y" without comment. What is the fault?
> It mixes a positive statement (the assessment) with a normative, prescriptive one (the recommendation). The step needs a criterion of evaluation that is named openly.
