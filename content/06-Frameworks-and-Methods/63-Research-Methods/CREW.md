> [!bluf]
> CREW breaks every argument into four parts, so you can build one or take one apart:
> - **Claim**: what you want the reader to believe.
> - **Reason**: why they should believe it.
> - **Evidence**: what the reason rests on.
> - **Warrant**: the general principle that connects the reason to the claim.
> 
> The core reads: **claim *because* reason, *based on* evidence**. A good argument adds a fifth part, **acknowledgment and response**: it names the reader's strongest objection and answers it.
> Use it when you write a paper, a policy brief or an assessment, and when you judge someone else's. Most weak arguments fail at the part nobody wrote down: the warrant.

## Purpose

An argument is not a quarrel. It is a **conversation with a critical reader** who cannot interrupt you. CREW turns the questions that reader would ask into a checklist. Each part answers one question:

| Part | The reader asks | Weak if … |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | *What do you want me to believe?* | vague, obvious or not contestable |
| **Reason** | *Why do you believe that?* | it only restates the claim in other words |
| **Evidence** | *What do you base that on?* | missing, anecdotal, unsourced or cherry-picked |
| **Warrant** | *How does that reason support that claim?* | unstated, false, or not accepted by the reader |
| *Acknowledgment & response* | *But what about …?* | the obvious objection is ignored |

The model comes from the philosopher **Stephen Toulmin**, who analysed how arguments actually work in law and everyday reasoning, not how formal logic says they should [@toulmin1958uses]. **Booth, Colomb, Williams et al.** adapted it for research writing in *The Craft of Research* [@booth2016craft]. *CREW* is a teaching mnemonic for their parts.

## Components / Steps

1. **Claim**
   
   The answer to your research question, stated as a sentence someone could reasonably deny.
   - **Specific:** "Sanctions failed" is weak. "Sanctions did not change the regime's behaviour, because its ruling coalition was insulated from their cost" is better.
   - **Significant:** it must matter to the reader. *So what?* is a fair question.
   - **Qualified:** limit the claim to what the evidence can carry: *usually*, *in most cases*, *under these conditions*. A modest claim that holds beats a bold claim that falls.
   
1. **Reasons**
   
   Your statements of *why* the claim should be accepted. Reasons are **made by you**: they are your thinking, not yet facts. A big claim usually needs several reasons, and each reason can itself become a sub-claim that needs its own support. That is how an argument grows into a paper.
   
1. **Evidence**
   
   What reasons rest on: data, documents, quotations, observations, cases. Evidence is **found, not made**. Booth et al. stress that the reader never sees the evidence itself, only your **report** of it. So the report must be checkable, and good evidence is:
   - **accurate** (correctly reported) and **precise** (no vaguer than needed),
   - **sufficient** (enough to carry the reason) and **representative** (not only the convenient cases),
   - **authoritative** (from a source the reader trusts, cited so they can check).
   
1. **Warrant**
   
   The general principle that makes the step from reason to claim legitimate. It has the form **"whenever X, then (generally) Y"**. The reason is a specific instance of X, the claim a specific instance of Y.
   - Warrants are usually **left unstated**, because writer and reader share them. That is fine until they don't. When the reader rejects your warrant, more evidence will not help: you have to state and defend the principle itself.
   - Check a warrant three ways: Is it **true**? Does the reason really fall under its *X*, and the claim under its *Y*? Is it **accepted** in the reader's field?
   
1. **Acknowledgment & response**
   
   Name the strongest objection, alternative explanation or limit, then answer it: concede, rebut or narrow the claim. This is not a weakness. It shows the reader you have seen what they see, and it is where most of an argument's credibility comes from.

### Toulmin's original terms

| Toulmin (1958) | CREW (Booth et al.) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | Claim | same |
| Data / grounds | Reasons + evidence | Booth splits Toulmin's *data* into the writer's reason and the reported evidence under it |
| Warrant | Warrant | same |
| Backing | Evidence *for* the warrant | why the warrant itself should be believed |
| Qualifier | Qualifications in the claim | *probably*, *usually*, *in most cases* |
| Rebuttal | Acknowledgment & response | conditions under which the claim would not hold |

## Worked Example

**Question:** Can the [[OODA Loop]] be used as a model for decision-making in a multinational coalition?

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | The OODA loop should **not** be applied unchanged to coalition decision-making; at most it explains the tempo of each member's own decisions. |
| **Reason 1** | The loop was built for a single decision-maker who controls their own orientation and action. |
| **Evidence 1** | [[John Boyd]] developed it from air-to-air combat, where one pilot observes, decides and acts [@osinga2007science]. |
| **Reason 2** | A coalition has no single orientation: members decide by consensus, and each has its own politics and caveats. |
| **Evidence 2** | Would need sources: a case of coalition decision-making (e.g. a NATO operation) that shows consensus rules and national caveats slowing decisions. |
| **Warrant** | *Whenever a model is used outside the subject and purpose it was built for, its conclusions lose validity.* This is Stachowiak's pragmatic feature ([[General Model Theory]]). |
| **Acknowledgment** | *But* Boyd himself extended the loop from pilots to whole armies and to grand strategy [@boyd2018discourse]. |
| **Response** | Those extensions still assume one command that can decide and act as a unit. That is exactly what a consensus coalition lacks. So the claim is narrowed, not dropped: OODA fits each member, not the coalition as a whole. |

The table shows two things. First, **Evidence 2 is still a gap**: the reason is plausible, but it is not yet supported. CREW makes that gap visible. Second, the **warrant** does most of the work. A reader who rejects Stachowiak's pragmatism (see the critiques in [[General Model Theory]]) will not be moved by more evidence about Boyd's biography.

## Strengths & Pitfalls

**Strengths**

- **A diagnostic tool.** Read any paper, brief or judgment and fill in the five rows. Empty rows show where it is weak.
- **Separates thinking from facts.** Reasons are the author's; evidence is the world's. Mixing them is a common way to present an opinion as a finding.
- **Makes hidden assumptions explicit.** Stating the warrant often reveals that the real disagreement is about a principle, not about the facts.
- **Scales.** A single paragraph and a whole thesis have the same structure: each reason becomes a sub-claim with its own reasons and evidence.

**Pitfalls**

- **Reason = claim in other words.** "The policy failed because it did not work" is circular.
- **Evidence without a report.** "Studies show …" gives the reader nothing to check.
- **Unshared warrants.** Arguing across disciplines or cultures (law vs economics, one strategic culture vs another) often fails because the warrant each side takes for granted is not shared.
- **Straw-man acknowledgment.** Answering a weak objection instead of the strongest one.

> [!counter] Critique
> - **Informal, not formal.** CREW describes how persuasion works for a reader. It does not prove validity the way formal logic does, and a well-structured argument can still be false.
> - **Warrants are hard to pin down.** Toulmin's own distinction between *data* and *warrant* is blurry in practice, and different readers reconstruct different warrants for the same argument.
> - **Assumes a reasonable reader.** The model presumes a reader who will be moved by reasons and evidence. In political or adversarial settings, arguments often fail for reasons the structure cannot capture: interest, identity, trust in the speaker.
> - **Built for Anglo-American writing.** The claim-first, explicitly argued style is a convention. Other academic traditions build up to the claim, or leave more implicit.

## Self-Test: CREW

> [!qard]- 1. Name the four parts of CREW and the reader's question each one answers.
> - **Claim:** *What do you want me to believe?*
> - **Reason:** *Why do you believe that?*
> - **Evidence:** *What do you base that on?*
> - **Warrant:** *How does that reason support that claim?*
> 
> Plus **acknowledgment & response:** *But what about …?*

> [!qard]- 2. What is the difference between a reason and evidence?
> A **reason** is the writer's own statement of why the claim holds; it is made by the writer. **Evidence** is found in the world (data, documents, cases) and reported to the reader. A reason without evidence is just another claim.

> [!qard]- 3. What is a warrant, and why is it the most common weak point?
> A general principle of the form *whenever X, then Y* that connects the reason to the claim. It is usually left unstated because writers assume the reader shares it. If the reader does not, no amount of evidence will convince them: the warrant itself must be stated and defended.

> [!qard]- 4. Find the missing warrant: "Country A will not attack, because it depends on trade with Country B."
> *Whenever a state depends heavily on trade with another, it will avoid war with it* (economic interdependence). A reader who knows 1914 (heavy trade between Britain and Germany, and war anyway) may reject this warrant, so it would need to be stated, qualified and defended.

> [!qard]- 5. Why should a good argument include acknowledgment and response?
> Because a critical reader will think of the objection anyway. Naming the strongest one and answering it (by conceding, rebutting or narrowing the claim) shows the argument has been tested and makes it more credible, not less.

> [!qard]- 6. Apply: diagnose this argument. "The reform failed because it did not work. Studies show this."
> The reason only restates the claim, so it is circular. The evidence is not a report the reader can check. No warrant is stated. Give a separate reason and cite the evidence.

> [!qard]- 7. Explain: the reader rejects your warrant. Why does more evidence not help, and what does?
> Evidence supports the reason, not the step from reason to claim. State the principle and defend it with backing of its own, or use a warrant that the reader's field accepts.
