> [!bluf]
> 
> The General Model Theory is the idea of Herbert Stachowiak that all cognition is cognition in models. Models are representations of "reality", which shorten what is represented.
> The respective shape of the model depends on the model creator, his historical and social situation as well as his cognitive or creative interests.
> 
> It is a structural way to think about models and how to analyse them: every model is a model **of something** (mapping), leaves things out **on purpose** (reduction), and is only valid **for someone, for a time and for a purpose** (pragmatism).

## Definition

A model is a simplifying image of reality.
According to Herbert Stachowiak, a model is characterized by at least three properties:

1. **Mapping** (*Abbildungsmerkmal*)
   
   A model always is a model of something - it is the representation of some natural or artificial, existing or imagined original, where this original itself could be a model.
1. **Reduction** (*Verkürzungsmerkmal*)
   
   In general, a model will not include all attributes that describe the original.
   The model is limited to the attributes that appear relevant to the model's creator or user.
   Thus simplifying the real source into a simpler abstraction.
   
1. **Pragmatism** (*pragmatisches Merkmal*)
   
   A model does not relate unambiguously to its original.
   It is intended to work as a replacement for the original.
   It is intended to take the place of the original for a limited use-case:
   - **for whom:** for a certain subject (the one who builds or uses the model)
   - **when:** for a certain time range
   - **what for:** restricted to a certain operation / action

## Key Points

- A model is a reduced abstraction / image of the original source.
- **Reduction is a feature, not a bug.** A map at a scale of 1:1 would be as useless as the terrain itself. A model is useful *because* it leaves things out.
- **The mapping is not one-to-one in either direction.** Stachowiak separates attributes of the original that are left out (**preterite** attributes, *präterierte Attribute*) from attributes the model has but the original does not (**abundant** attributes, *abundante Attribute*): the grid on a map, the colours of countries on a political map, the arrows in a diagram. Mistaking abundant attributes for reality is a classic error.
- **No model is simply "true".** A model can only be judged against its purpose: *good for whom, when, for what?* Stachowiak's theory is openly pragmatist on this point.
- **Models form chains.** The original can itself be a model: terrain → reconnaissance report → map → briefing sketch → the commander's mental picture. Every step reduces again, and every step reflects someone's purpose.
- **Mental models count too.** "All cognition is cognition in models": perception, a situation picture (*Lagebild*) or a doctrine are models in the same sense as a map or an equation.
- **The four analysis questions.** To analyse any model, ask: *Of what?* *What is kept, what is left out, what is added?* *For whom and when?* *What for?* Most misuse of a model is use outside its pragmatic limits.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Herbert Stachowiak** (1921–2004), a German philosopher, developed the theory against a background of **cybernetics** and **pragmatist epistemology**. The standard text is *Allgemeine Modelltheorie* (1973).
2. **The core claim is epistemological**, not technical: we never deal with reality directly, only with models of it. So the question is never whether a model is "the truth", but whether it serves its user for a purpose.
3. **Related ideas** make the same point from other angles: Korzybski's *"the map is not the territory"* (1933), and the statistician George Box's *"all models are wrong, but some are useful"* (1979).
4. **Reception:** the three features became a standard definition of "model" in German-speaking academia, especially in **computer science** (conceptual modelling, model-driven engineering), **didactics** and **management and leadership education**, where it is taught as a tool for handling models critically.

## Application

### Analysing a model (exam method)

| Question | Feature | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| A model *of what*? | Mapping | Name the original. Is the original itself already a model? |
| What is kept, left out, added? | Reduction | Which attributes are relevant, which are ignored (preterite), which are artefacts of the model (abundant)? |
| For whom, when, what for? | Pragmatism | Who built it, in what era, for which decision? Is it being used outside those limits? |

### Worked example: the OODA loop as a model

- **Mapping:** the original is decision-making in competition, first a fighter pilot's in air combat, later organisations and states. See [[OODA Loop]].
- **Reduction:** four stages and a few feedback loops. Emotion, politics, coalition friction and bureaucracy are left out. Abundant: the neat boxes and arrows suggest a clean sequence that real decisions do not have.
- **Pragmatism:** built by [[John Boyd]] for military audiences, 1970s–90s, to argue for manoeuvre over attrition. The standard critique of OODA (*"transfer from air combat"*) is exactly a **pragmatic** critique: the model is used beyond the subject and purpose it was built for.

### Leadership and command

- **Orientation is a mental model.** Boyd's *Orient* is the place where a commander's model of the situation is built. His essay *Destruction and Creation* says the same as Stachowiak's pragmatism in other words: a model that has outlived its time or purpose must be broken up and rebuilt.
- **Doctrine is a model** of how to fight: reduced to principles, written for a specific army, era and threat. Outdated doctrine is a model used beyond its time range.
- **Plans, maps, briefings and org charts** are all models. A leader should ask what each one hides, and who it was made for, before acting on it.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Too broad to be useful?** If *all* cognition is cognition in models, the term "model" risks losing its contrast with theory, concept or plain perception.
> - **What is the "original"?** If we only ever know models, the original is itself only accessible through models. Realists object that Stachowiak's pragmatism evades the question of truth instead of answering it.
> - **Necessary, not sufficient:** the three features say what a model *is*, not what makes a model *good*. They give no criteria for validity or for how much reduction is too much.
> - **Mapping is hard to check for mental models,** where the original (a situation, an intention, an enemy's plan) is not directly observable.

## Self-Test: GMT

> [!qard]- 1. Name and explain Stachowiak's three features of a model.
> - **Mapping:** a model is always a model *of something*, an original, which may itself be a model.
> - **Reduction:** it keeps only the attributes that seem relevant to its creator or user.
> - **Pragmatism:** it replaces the original only *for someone*, *for a time* and *for a purpose*.

> [!qard]- 2. Why is reduction not a weakness of a model?
> Because it is what makes the model usable. A model that kept every attribute would be as complex as the original and would help no one decide anything. The question is not *whether* to reduce, but whether the right attributes were kept for the purpose.

> [!qard]- 3. What are preterite and abundant attributes? Give an example.
> **Preterite** attributes exist in the original but are left out of the model (the colour of buildings is missing from a military map). **Abundant** attributes exist in the model but not in the original (the coordinate grid on a map). Both show that model and original are not one-to-one.

> [!qard]- 4. Apply the three features to the OODA loop.
> - **Mapping:** decision-making in competition, originally air combat.
> - **Reduction:** four stages and feedback loops, without emotion, politics or organisational friction.
> - **Pragmatism:** made by Boyd for military officers, to argue for manoeuvre warfare. Using it for a coalition's policy process stretches it beyond its original purpose.

> [!qard]- 5. Why does General Model Theory matter for a leader?
> Everything a leader acts on (maps, reports, plans, doctrine, their own situation picture) is a model. Knowing that each one is reduced and made for a purpose makes a leader ask what is missing and whether the model still fits the current situation, time and task.

> [!qard]- 6. Apply: a unit plans from a doctrine regulation written twenty years ago. Describe the problem in Stachowiak's terms.
> A failure of the pragmatic feature. Doctrine is a model built for a certain army, era and threat. Used beyond its time range, it no longer stands in for the original.

> [!qard]- 7. Limit: the three features say what a model is. What do they not say?
> What makes a model *good*. They give no criterion for validity or for how much reduction is too much. A model can only be judged against its purpose.
