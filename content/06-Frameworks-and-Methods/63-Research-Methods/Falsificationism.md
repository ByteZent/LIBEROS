> [!bluf]
> Two answers to the question of how science reaches knowledge:
> - **Logical empiricism** says: collect observations and **verify** general statements by them (**induction**: from single cases to the rule).
> - **Falsificationism** says: no number of observations can prove a general statement, but **one** can refute it. So state a bold theory, **deduce** what must be observed if it is true, and try to prove it wrong.
>
> A statement is scientific only if it is **falsifiable**: some possible observation would contradict it. A theory that fits every possible fact says nothing.

## Definition

| | Logical empiricism | Falsificationism |
|---|---|---|
| **Aim** | Verification: confirm a statement by sense experience | Falsification: try to refute a statement |
| **Direction** | **Induction**: single observations (*Protokollsätze*) → general statement | **Deduction**: general statement → single predictions that are tested |
| **Good theory** | One confirmed by many observations | One that forbids much and has survived serious attempts to refute it |
| **Status of knowledge** | Can be established | Always provisional |

**Example.**
- *Inductive:* camouflage uniforms reduced detection in an exercise in Switzerland. They did so in an exercise in Germany. Therefore: "If camouflage uniforms are used, detection by the enemy falls."
- *Deductive:* take that general statement and derive tests. Does it hold in a forest? In another army? In the mountains? One clear failure (no reduction in the mountains) refutes the general statement as it stands.

## Key Points

- **The induction problem.** A turkey is fed every evening at seven and concludes "I am always fed at seven". The rule holds until the day before the feast. No finite series of cases guarantees the next one.
- **Observation is not a neutral base.**
    - Facts are often only accessible through theoretical concepts.
    - Protocol sentences can be false.
    - Background assumptions shape what is seen: Ptolemy sees the sun rise, Copernicus sees the horizon fall. This is the **theory-ladenness of observation** [@hanson1958patterns].
- **Falsifiable does not mean falsified.** It is enough that a refuting situation is logically conceivable. "Ravens are black" is falsifiable: a white raven would refute it. "God created the world exactly as it is" is not: every fact confirms it by definition.
- **Two further conditions.** The theory must be logically consistent. And a theory that has already been falsified is falsifiable but no longer acceptable.
- **The more a theory says, the better.** High **information content** means it forbids many possible observations and is therefore easier to refute.
- **No ad hoc hypotheses.** A theory must not be saved from refutation by adding an excuse that has no other purpose.
- **Null hypothesis.** Because a hypothesis cannot be verified, research states its negation and tries to reject that. Rejecting the null hypothesis strengthens the original hypothesis indirectly. It does not prove it.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Logical empiricism** (Vienna Circle, 1920s and 1930s): only statements that can be verified by experience are meaningful.
2. **Popper** turned this around: science is marked by falsifiability, and it proceeds by conjecture and refutation [@popper1959logic; @popper1963conjectures].
3. **Kuhn** objected that scientists in practice do not give up a theory after one refutation. They work inside a paradigm and change it only in rare crises [@kuhn1962structure].

## Application

- **Writing a hypothesis.** Ask: which observation would show that I am wrong? If there is none, it is not a hypothesis (see [[Research Design]]).
- **Judging a theory.** Popper's standard example is Freud: whatever a person does can be explained in Freudian terms, so no behaviour can contradict the theory (see [[Menschenbilder in Psychology]]).
- **Intelligence and staff work.** An assessment that is compatible with every enemy action is worthless for planning. A good one names the indicators that would show it to be wrong. Looking only for confirming evidence is confirmation bias (see [[Bias in Research]]).

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **A single test never hits a single hypothesis.** Every test also relies on auxiliary assumptions (the instrument works, the sample is sound). When a prediction fails, logic does not say which part is wrong.
> - **Social science hypotheses are probabilistic.** "The older, the more likely to vote" is not refuted by one young voter. Falsification becomes a matter of statistical convention, not of logic.
> - **Not how science actually works.** Researchers defend theories against anomalies for a long time, often rightly.
> - **Falsification rests on observation too.** If observation is theory-laden, the refuting observation is as uncertain as the confirming one.
> - **Where do theories come from?** Falsificationism says how to test a theory, not how to find one. In practice exploration and induction supply the ideas.

## Self-Test: Falsificationism

> [!qard]- 1. What is the induction problem?
> A general statement cannot be logically derived from any finite number of single observations. The next case may differ (the turkey).

> [!qard]- 2. Induction or deduction: which belongs to which position?
> Induction to logical empiricism (from observations to the rule). Deduction to falsificationism (from the rule to testable predictions).

> [!qard]- 3. Give one falsifiable and one unfalsifiable statement.
> Falsifiable: "All ravens are black." Unfalsifiable: "God created the world exactly as it is."

> [!qard]- 4. Does "falsifiable" mean the theory has been refuted?
> No. It means a refuting observation is logically conceivable.

> [!qard]- 5. Why is a theory with higher information content better for a falsificationist?
> It forbids more, so it risks more, and if it survives testing we have learned more.

> [!qard]- 6. What is an ad hoc hypothesis and why is it not allowed?
> An extra assumption added only to rescue a theory from a refutation. It lowers the content of the theory and makes it immune to testing.

> [!qard]- 7. Apply: an assessment reads "the enemy may attack or may hold, depending on circumstances." Judge it as a falsificationist.
> It is worthless for planning. It fits every enemy action, so it forbids nothing and says nothing. A good assessment names the indicators that would show it to be wrong.

> [!qard]- 8. Limit: a prediction fails. Why does that not strictly refute the hypothesis?
> Every test also rests on auxiliary assumptions (the instrument, the sample), and logic does not say which part is wrong. Social science hypotheses are also probabilistic, so one counter-case refutes nothing.
