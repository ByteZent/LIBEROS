*"Motherland of modernity?" A cultural and social history of Great Britain, c. 1780–1930* (Prof. Harald Fischer-Tiné, D-GESS; Wednesday 14:15–15:45, ML E 12). The lecture uses Britain as a case study for the transformations that every modernising society has gone through. The aim is not to learn the "facts" of British history but to become sensitive to the central historical debates about modernity and modernisation. The weight lies on topics that historians dispute.

## Scope

- **Time:** a *longue durée* view from the late 18th century to the first third of the 20th. The focus is the "age of transformation", 1830–1914.
- **Space:** England, with occasional excursions to the rest of the UK (Ireland, Scotland). The Empire appears only indirectly, through its effects on British society and politics. Less breadth buys more depth.
- **Approach:** social, cultural, economic history and the history of ideas. Diplomatic, political and military history is only touched on.
- **Each session:** revision (about 10 min), lecture (about 60 min), then questions, film clips and source analysis (about 20 min). Slides appear on Moodle a few days after the lecture (the slides say three days, the syllabus one week).

**Key aspects of (Western) modernity** named in lecture 1, each a candidate for a note and a thread through the sessions:

- industrialisation
- flight from the land and urbanisation
- mass production and the consumer society
- the spread of science and technology
- more mobility and denser communication, leading to globalisation
- nationalism and the homogenising nation state
- bureaucratisation and a state with more power to intervene
- the growing weight of the public sphere and mass media
- the trend towards mass politics and democracy
- civil society as a buffer between the individual and the state
- secularisation and scientific rationality
- gender and class hierarchies being fixed and eroded at the same time

## Session 1: Britain in the world, 1780–1939

What the first lecture covered. The same events sit on the timeline: [[Britain 1780–1939 (Timeline)]].

**Units that are often confused:** England · Great Britain · United Kingdom · British Empire · British Commonwealth.

**Inward-facing imperialism.** For three centuries England was at war with Catholic powers (Spain, France). It integrated the mostly Catholic "Celtic Fringe": 1536 Wales annexed, 1707 union with Scotland, 1801 union with Ireland and the creation of the United Kingdom.

**The Empire.** Overseas expansion begins in the early 17th century in North America and the Caribbean (the "First Empire"), which ends in 1783 with the loss of the American colonies. A second wave from 1784 to 1914 in Asia, Africa and the Pacific builds the largest territorial empire in history (the "Second Empire"), at its greatest extent in 1921. The Balfour formula of 1926 defines the UK and the Dominions as autonomous and equal communities in the British Commonwealth of Nations.

| Period | What happens | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1793–1815 · wars against France | Britain ends the conflict as the unchallenged ruler of the seas. Permanent war turns it into a "fiscal-military state": professional administration (census from 1801, civil service 1870) and wider taxation that pays for the army and navy | Rise to world power |
| 1815–1873 · age of free trade | Peace and free-trade liberalism guide foreign policy: balance of power, an overseas orientation and "splendid isolation" from Europe. Peace in Europe is broken only by the Crimean War (1854–56), alongside some 200 colonial wars. Palmerston is the architect | World-power status secured by (gunboat) diplomacy |
| 1874–1914 · European rivalries and "New Imperialism" | Germany, France and the USA challenge Britain's economic and political lead. Scramble for Africa from the 1880s. Naval Defence Act 1889 and the two-power standard. The Boer War (1899–1902) is the first great "imperial war", with troops from the Dominions, and splits the public into "jingoists" and "Little Englanders" | Arms race, debates on "national efficiency" and self-sufficiency |
| 1914–1939 · First World War and the interwar years | The war arrives in the middle of internal social conflicts. About 740,000 dead (1.1 million with Empire troops) and 2.5 million wounded. The war drives social protest and democratisation at home (General Strike 1926) and decolonisation in the Empire. US entry and the Russian Revolution of 1917 change the geopolitical order. Britain fights communism to defend the Empire and at first resists Nazi Germany only half-heartedly (appeasement, 1938) | A drained country |
| 1939–1947 · outlook | Britain wins the Second World War but is humiliated by Japan and nearly occupied by Germany. In 1945 it is economically drained and politically weakened. India's independence in 1947 starts the break-up of the Empire | Superpower status lost for good |

## Glossary (for the German exam)

| Deutsch | English | Definition | Be able to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moderne / Modernisierung | Modernity / modernisation | The bundle of linked changes (industry, cities, mass politics, science, the state) that the course studies in the British case. | apply |
| Mutterland der Moderne | Motherland of modernity | The claim, put as a question by the course, that Britain was the first modern society and a model for others. | apply |
| Landflucht | Flight from the land | Migration from the countryside to the towns that feeds urbanisation. | define |
| Konsumgesellschaft | Consumer society | A society in which mass-produced goods and leisure are available to broad groups and shape identity. | define |
| Säkularisierung | Secularisation | The declining authority of religion in public life and thought, with scientific rationality taking its place. | apply |
| Zivilgesellschaft | Civil society | Associations and movements that stand between the individual and the state. | define |
| Celtic Fringe | Celtic Fringe | Wales, Scotland and Ireland as the periphery that England integrated between 1536 and 1801. | define |
| Fiscal-military state | Fiscal-military state | A state reshaped by permanent war: higher taxes and a professional administration pay for the army and navy. | apply |
| Freihandel | Free trade | Britain's guiding economic and foreign-policy principle from 1815 to 1873. | define |
| Splendid isolation | Splendid isolation | Britain's relative distance from European alliances while it looked overseas. | define |
| Kanonenbootdiplomatie | Gunboat diplomacy | Securing interests by showing or threatening naval force. | define |
| Wettlauf um Afrika | Scramble for Africa | The partition of Africa among the European powers from the 1880s. | define |
| Two-power standard | Two-power standard | The rule of the Naval Defence Act 1889: the navy must match the next two largest navies together. | define |
| Jingoists / Little Englanders | Jingoists / Little Englanders | Aggressive supporters of empire, and its critics who wanted Britain to turn back to itself. A split made visible by the Boer War. | apply |
