> [!bluf]
> **Cognitive warfare** is the deliberate attempt to change **how** a target population or its decision-makers think, not only **what** they know, in order to gain a strategic advantage, often without firing a shot and without the target noticing. It goes beyond propaganda and information warfare. Its object is the **cognitive process itself**: attention, perception, trust, biases and group identity. It uses social media, data-driven targeting, AI-generated content and insights from neuroscience and psychology.
>
> The term was popularised in **NATO** from 2020. It builds on older ideas: Soviet and Russian **reflexive control** and China's **"three warfares"** and **cognitive domain operations**. Its strength as a concept is that it points to real vulnerabilities of open societies. Its weakness is that it is **vague and easily inflated**: if every opinion contest counts as "warfare", the concept loses its edge and can justify restricting free debate.

## Definition

NATO has no single agreed definition. Two influential early versions:

- **Bernal et al. (2020)**, written for NATO: cognitive warfare is the **weaponisation of public opinion** by an outside actor, aiming to influence public or government policy and to destabilise public institutions [@bernal2020cognitive].
- **du Cluzel (2021)**, NATO Innovation Hub: cognitive warfare uses technology to **alter the cognition of human targets**, usually without their awareness. It makes the human mind a contested space in its own right, alongside land, sea, air, space and cyber [@ducluzel2021cognitive].

**In my words:** information warfare fights over the *messages*; cognitive warfare fights over the *receiver*. It tries to shape the filters through which people take in every message, so that the target reaches the conclusions the attacker wants on its own.

## Key Points

- **Target: the process, not the content.** Classic propaganda tries to convince. Cognitive warfare tries to **confuse, polarise, exhaust and erode trust**, so that a society cannot form a shared picture of reality or decide together. Being believed is optional; being doubted is often enough.
- **It exploits normal cognition.** Its tools are the ordinary shortcuts of human thinking: confirmation bias, the availability of vivid examples, trust in one's own group, emotional arousal, and repetition making claims feel true [@kahneman2011thinking]. Nobody has to be "irrational" for it to work.
- **Whole of society.** Soldiers are targets, but so are voters, journalists, officials and families. The front line runs through every smartphone.
- **Permanent and below the threshold.** It runs in peacetime, below the level of armed attack, which makes it part of **hybrid threats**. Attribution is hard, and responses are politically costly.
- **Technology multiplies it.** Platforms that sort content by engagement, detailed personal data for **micro-targeting**, bots and troll networks, and **generative AI** (fake text, images, voices and video) lower the cost and raise the scale.
- **It aims at orientation.** In [[OODA Loop|Boyd's]] terms, cognitive warfare attacks the *Orient* step directly: it tries to corrupt the mental models through which an opponent interprets everything else. It is the logic of [[Attrition vs Manoeuvre Warfare|manoeuvre]] carried into the mind: break cohesion, not material.

### How it differs from related concepts

| Concept | Main object | Typical actors | Level |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Propaganda** | beliefs and attitudes, through messages | states, parties | strategic |
| **Psychological operations (PSYOPS)** | the behaviour of specific audiences in an operation | military units | operational to tactical |
| **Information warfare / operations** | information and information systems (content *and* networks, incl. cyber) | military, intelligence | all |
| **Disinformation** | specific false or misleading content, spread on purpose | many | a *tool* |
| **Cognitive warfare** | the cognitive processes of whole populations and decision-makers | states and proxies, often covert | strategic, long-term |

The boundaries are blurred. Critics argue that cognitive warfare is information warfare with a new label and new tools (see the critique below).

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Reflexive control (Soviet Union, from the 1960s).** The idea of giving an opponent information that leads him to **choose, of his own will, the decision you want**. It remains central to Russian thinking on *information confrontation* [@thomas2004russia].
2. **China's "three warfares" (2003).** The People's Liberation Army's political work regulations name **public opinion warfare, psychological warfare and legal warfare**. Later PLA writing speaks of **cognitive domain operations** and treats "winning minds" as part of *intelligentised* warfare. Taiwan is the most intensely targeted case [@hung2022china].
3. **Russian interference in the 2016 US election.** The Internet Research Agency's social media campaign became the reference case for large-scale, platform-based influence [@mueller2019report].
4. **NATO (from 2020).** Studies for NATO's Innovation Hub popularised the term and asked whether the mind should be treated as a **sixth operational domain** [@bernal2020cognitive; @ducluzel2021cognitive]. NATO has since worked on cognitive warfare as a concept, but has not adopted a separate cognitive domain.
5. **Ukraine since 2022.** A deepfake video of President Zelensky calling on troops to surrender (March 2022) was quickly exposed, and showed both the new tool and the value of prepared resilience.

## Application

### Defence: cognitive resilience

Because the targets are whole societies, defence is mainly **societal**, not military:

- **Media and information literacy** from school onwards (Finland is the usual example).
- **Trusted institutions and independent media**: the best protection against *doubt* is institutions people have reason to trust.
- **Rapid, transparent communication** by authorities: pre-empting and exposing false narratives (*prebunking*) instead of only reacting.
- **Dedicated agencies**: e.g. Sweden's **Psychological Defence Agency** (2022), which supports society's ability to resist influence while protecting free debate.
- **Platform regulation and transparency** about political advertising, bots and recommendation systems.

### Swiss angle

Switzerland had a historic form of cognitive defence long before the term existed: the **Geistige Landesverteidigung** (*spiritual national defence*) of the late 1930s, a state-backed effort to strengthen a shared Swiss identity against National Socialist and Fascist propaganda. It is a useful but double-edged precedent: it strengthened cohesion, but it also narrowed debate and was used against dissenters well into the Cold War. Today the Federal Council's security policy reports name disinformation and influence activities as threats, and the militia and direct democracy make Swiss society both **resilient** (high trust, local deliberation) and **exposed** (frequent votes are attractive targets).

> [!assessment] Why direct democracy is both a target and a defence
> Frequent popular votes give an outside actor many concrete occasions to intervene. But they also train citizens in reading arguments, receiving official voting booklets, and debating locally, which builds exactly the resilience cognitive warfare tries to erode. *(My own judgement.)*

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Conceptual inflation.** If shaping opinion counts as warfare, then advertising, diplomacy and domestic politics become "war" too. The label can **securitise** normal political debate and justify censorship or surveillance of one's own citizens.
> - **Old wine, new bottles.** Much of what is described (propaganda, deception, reflexive control, PSYOPS) is old. What is new is scale, speed and data. Whether that justifies a new concept, or a new *domain*, is contested.
> - **Effects are often overstated.** Research on persuasion suggests people are **harder to fool** than alarmist accounts assume: they filter messages by plausibility and trust, and most disinformation reaches people who already agree with it [@mercier2020not]. The measurable electoral effect of campaigns like 2016 is disputed.
> - **Offensive use is ethically fraught.** For democracies, deliberately manipulating cognition, even of an adversary's population, conflicts with the values being defended, and the effects spill back home through shared platforms.
> - **Neuro-hype.** Claims about "hacking the brain" often run far ahead of what neuroscience can actually do.

## Self-Test: CogWar

> [!qard]- 1. Define cognitive warfare and say how it differs from information warfare.
> The deliberate attempt to change *how* a population or its leaders think (attention, trust, biases, identity) for strategic advantage, usually covertly. Information warfare focuses on information and information systems (messages, networks); cognitive warfare targets the receiver's cognitive processes.

> [!qard]- 2. What is reflexive control?
> A Soviet/Russian concept: feeding an opponent information designed to make him choose, apparently of his own will, the decision you want. It is a forerunner of cognitive warfare.

> [!qard]- 3. Why is doubt often a more effective aim than persuasion?
> Persuading people against their existing views is hard. Eroding trust in institutions, media and facts is easier, and a society that cannot agree on what is true cannot decide or act together.

> [!qard]- 4. Name three defences against cognitive warfare.
> Media and information literacy; trusted institutions and independent media with transparent, fast communication (incl. prebunking); dedicated civil agencies and platform transparency.

> [!qard]- 5. Give the strongest critique of the concept.
> Conceptual inflation: calling all influence "warfare" securitises normal debate and can justify restricting free speech, while research suggests the effects of disinformation are often overstated.

> [!qard]- 6. Apply: before a popular vote, anonymous accounts flood social media with contradictory claims about the proposal. What is the aim, and what is the right defence?
> The aim is doubt, polarisation and exhaustion, not belief: a society without a shared picture cannot decide. The defence is societal: early, transparent communication by trusted authorities (*prebunking*), media literacy, independent media.

> [!qard]- 7. Compare: cognitive warfare, PSYOPS and disinformation.
> PSYOPS aims at the behaviour of specific audiences in an operation. Disinformation is a *tool*: specific false content spread on purpose. Cognitive warfare aims at the thinking process of whole populations, long-term and often covertly.
