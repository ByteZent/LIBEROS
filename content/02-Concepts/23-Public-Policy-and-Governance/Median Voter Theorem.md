> [!bluf]
> The median voter is the voter in the **middle** of the electorate on one policy dimension: half want more, half want less. Under majority rule a position at the median beats every other, so politicians who want to win move there, and policy follows the middle of the electorate.
>
> The theorem does two jobs in the course. It explains why **democracies and autocracies make different policy**: in an autocracy only a small elite counts, and its median lies elsewhere than that of the population. And it explains why **two parties move to the centre** and come to resemble each other.
>
> It holds only under strong assumptions: one policy dimension and everyone votes, and for the party version two parties as well. Where these fail, policy can sit far from the median.

## Definition

The median voter is the person whose preference in a policy field lies exactly in the middle of the spectrum of all those entitled to vote: one half wants more, the other half less [@bernauer2025einfuhrung].

The theorem: if voters can be lined up on one dimension and each votes for the option closest to their own position, the position of the median voter wins a majority against every alternative.

**In my words:** whoever stands in the middle has a majority behind them in both directions. A proposal to the left of the middle loses everyone to the right of it plus the middle. So the middle decides.

![[median-voter-democracy-autocracy.svg]]

**A small example** (my own). Five voters want 2, 4, 5, 7 and 9 billion francs for education. The median is 5.

| Vote | For the median (5) | For the alternative | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 against 7 | the voters at 2, 4, 5 | the voters at 7, 9 | 5 wins 3 : 2 |
| 5 against 4 | the voters at 5, 7, 9 | the voters at 2, 4 | 5 wins 3 : 2 |

Whatever is put against 5, the median voter and everyone on the other side from the alternative vote for 5, and that is always a majority. Note that the median (5) is not the average (5.4): one voter who wants 90 billion moves the average, not the median.

## Key Points

The theorem comes in two versions. The first is about **what a majority decides**, the second about **where parties stand**.

### Version 1: regime type and policy (ch. 3)

- **Whose middle?** Every political system has a median, but of different groups. In a democracy it is the median of all voters. In an autocracy it is the median of the small, privileged elite that has a say.
- **The gap is what matters.** The further the two medians lie apart in a policy field, the more the policies of democracies and autocracies differ in it. The lecture's example is spending on education: the elite's median wants less than the population's.
- **Welfare spending.** The democratic median voter has a lower income than the median of an autocratic elite, and so values redistribution from rich to poor more. Politicians who follow that voter spend more on welfare.

### Version 2: party competition (ch. 7)

- **Two parties converge.** With two parties on one dimension, each party gains votes by moving towards the middle, and the other follows. In equilibrium both stand close to the median and differ only in nuances. Neither can improve by moving away.
- **Assumptions** of the party version:
    1. Rational actors with single-peaked preferences on **one** dimension. Voters choose the nearest party, parties maximise their share of the vote.
    2. No stable party loyalties: voters calculate anew at every election.
    3. A two-party system with majority voting.
- **The first version needs less:** one dimension, single-peaked preferences and majority rule are enough. It needs no parties at all.

## Origins & Evolution

- The idea goes back to Hotelling's model of two shops on one street and to Black's work on committees. Downs turned it into a theory of party competition [@downs1957economic]. This history is from general knowledge, not from the course.
- The textbook uses it twice: in ch. 3 to compare democracies and autocracies, and in ch. 7 for party positions [@bernauer2025einfuhrung].

## Application

- **Regime type and policy.** Ask who has a say, find the middle of that group, and compare it with the middle of the population. That predicts where an autocracy's policy departs from what a democracy would do.
- **Reading party strategy.** "New Labour" under Blair is the textbook case of a party moving to the centre under majority voting.
- **Security policy.** For a referendum on armament, the theorem says the proposal that passes is the one the median voter prefers to the status quo, not the one the government or the army thinks best. That links it to veto players (see [[Democratic State]]).

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Not everyone votes.** Poorer and less educated citizens take part much less. Governments then follow the median of the middle and upper classes, not of the whole population.
> - **Politics has more than one dimension.** With two or more dimensions there is in general no stable median position.
> - **Organised interests.** Well-organised groups can obtain decisions that depart from the median voter's wishes (ch. 8).
> - **More than two parties.** Under proportional representation parties need not move to the centre. They can hold a position and enter a coalition.
> - **The middle need not be crowded.** The textbook pictures most voters in the middle. The theorem does not need that: the median wins under majority rule even when the electorate is split into two camps. But then both camps are unhappy with the result, and parties have more to gain from mobilising their own side than from moving to the centre. The second half is my own reasoning.
> - **Education as a counter-example.** Democracies do not simply spend more on primary schools, as the theorem suggests. The politically active middle class prefers secondary and higher education.

## Key Connections

- [[Democratic State]]: the theorem explains why elections discipline governments: straying from the middle costs the majority. Its comparison with selectorate theory is there: the theorem asks *who* decides, selectorate theory asks *how dear* loyalty is.
- [[Selectorate Theory]]: the second explanation of why democracies provide more public goods. It rests on different costs, the median voter theorem on different preferences.
- [[Autocratic State]]: in an autocracy the median that counts is the elite's.
- [[Separation of Powers]]: the theorem assumes one decision by majority. Every further veto player moves the result away from the median voter's ideal point and towards the status quo.
- [[Supply and Demand]]: the same kind of equilibrium reasoning, applied to votes instead of prices.

## Self-Test: Median Voter

> [!qard]- 1. Who is the median voter?
> The voter whose position on one policy dimension lies exactly in the middle of the electorate: half want more, half want less.

> [!qard]- 2. Why does the median position win under majority rule?
> Any other proposal loses the median voter and everyone on the other side of it, which is a majority.

> [!qard]- 3. How does the theorem explain the difference between democratic and autocratic policy?
> In a democracy policy follows the median of all voters, in an autocracy the median of a small elite. The larger the gap between the two in a policy field, the more the policies differ.

> [!qard]- 4. Name the three assumptions of the party version.
> Rational actors with single-peaked preferences on one dimension, no stable party loyalties, and a two-party system with majority voting.

> [!qard]- 5. Diagram: name (1), (2) and (3).
> ![[median-voter-democracy-autocracy.svg]]

> (1) The median voter of the whole electorate. (2) The median of the elite. (3) The gap between the two medians, which decides how far the policies differ.

> [!qard]- 6. Apply: why do democracies spend more on social welfare?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> The democratic median voter has a lower income than the median of an autocratic elite and values redistribution more. Politicians who follow that voter spend more on welfare.

> [!qard]- 7. Limit: why can policy depart from the median voter even in a full democracy?
> Poorer and less educated citizens vote less, so the median of those who vote lies higher. Organised interest groups and a second policy dimension pull policy away as well.

> [!qard]- 8. Calculate: five voters want 1, 3, 4, 8 and 14 billion for the army. Which amount beats every other in a majority vote, and what is the average?
> 4 billion (the median). The average is 6 billion.
> <!-- qard-solution -->
> 1. Order the positions: 1, 3, 4, 8, 14. The middle one is 4.
> 2. Against any higher amount, the voters at 1, 3 and 4 vote for 4. Against any lower amount, the voters at 4, 8 and 14 do.
> 3. Average: (1 + 3 + 4 + 8 + 14) ÷ 5 = 6. The voter at 14 pulls the average up, not the median.

> [!qard]- 9. Compare: what does the party version of the theorem need that the regime version does not?
> Two parties that maximise votes under majority voting, and voters without fixed party loyalties. The regime version needs only one dimension, single-peaked preferences and majority rule.

> [!qard]- 10. Why is the median party called the kingmaker?
> It contains the median member of parliament. Neither the parties to its left nor those to its right have a majority without it, so it chooses its partner and shapes the content.

> [!qard]- 11. How does collective action theory qualify the theorem?
> Well-organised groups that can impose costs obtain decisions that depart from what the median voter wants. Rokkan: "votes count, but resources decide".
