> [!bluf]
> Democratisation is the change of a political system towards democracy. Political systems are mostly stable, so the question is why some states become more democratic and others fall back.
>
> **Modernisation theory** starts from a finding: rich countries are more often democracies. It has two readings:
> - **endogenous thesis** says wealth *creates* democracy: development brings industry, education and a middle class that demands a say
> - **exogenous thesis** says democracy arises for other reasons, and wealth *keeps* it alive
>
> The evidence for the endogenous thesis is weak, for the exogenous one good. Modernisation does not work automatically: it depends on what kind of development takes place and on whether the middle class is independent of the regime.

## Definition

**Democratisation:** the transition of a political system from autocracy towards democracy. The opposite movement is a relapse into autocracy.

**Modernisation theory** (Lipset): economic development leads, through a change in the structure of the economy and of society, to demands for political participation and so to democracy [@lipset1959some].

**In my words:** both readings agree that rich democracies are common. They disagree about the direction of the story. In one, money makes people ask for a vote. In the other, people get the vote for some other reason, and money is why nobody takes it away again.

## Key Points

### Lipset's causal chain

1. Economic development changes the **structure of the economy**: less agriculture, more industry and services.
2. That raises the demand for skilled labour: **education** rises, with urbanisation and infrastructure.
3. The **structure of society** changes: a growing middle class appears, and wealth shifts from the owners of land to the owners of capital.
4. These groups demand **political participation**. Pressure on the autocratic elite grows until it is overthrown or agrees to democratise.

### Two readings

| | Endogenous thesis | Exogenous thesis |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | Development **leads to** democratisation | Democracy arises for other reasons (collapse of the regime, war, pressure from outside). A high enough level of development **prevents its breakdown** |
| **In one line** | Wealth creates democracy | Wealth keeps democracy |
| **Mechanism** | Lipset's chain | Above a certain level of wealth, setting up an autocracy by force costs the elites more than what they keep after taxes in a democracy |
| **Authors** | Lipset | Przeworski and Limongi [@przeworski1997modernization] |
| **Evidence** | Weak: the effect largely disappears once historical differences between countries are taken into account [@acemoglu2008income] | Good: above a certain level of income, democracies hardly ever break down |

The research is not finished. Both theses are hard to test: political systems change rarely and are path-dependent, so there are few cases and many possible causes.

### What the exogenous thesis does and does not say

- It says nothing about **when** a democracy arises. That can happen at any level of income.
- It predicts **which** democracies last: the rich ones. Poor democracies are fragile.
- So the correlation between wealth and democracy comes from survival: democracies appear in poor and in rich countries, but only the rich ones are still there later. This is a selection effect (see [[Bias in Research]] on survivorship).

The last point is my own way of putting the argument.

### Weak points of Lipset's version

- It rests only on democratisations and leaves out relapses from democracy into autocracy.
- The correlation between wealth and democracy can arise in another way: wealth stabilises democracies that already exist.
- Development and the political system may both be shaped by historical and social factors. Then the correlation is spurious (see [[Bias in Research]] on confounding).

### Three cases

| | Taiwan / South Korea | China | Russia |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Economic development** | Rapid industrialisation, 1960s to 1980s | Strong growth since the 1980s, a large middle class | 1990s: deep economic crisis. 2000s: growth mainly from oil and gas |
| **Democratisation** | Yes, at the end of the 1980s | Not to this day | Yes in the 1990s, then a relapse |
| **Why?** | Broad industrialisation created an educated middle class that did not depend on the regime and pressed for a say | The middle class owes its rise to the party-state and is tied to it. The regime claims legitimacy from growth itself | Democracy came with the collapse of the Soviet Union, not from development. It then met a deep crisis, and later wealth came from oil and gas, which the state controls |
| **Fits …** | the endogenous thesis | Against the endogenous thesis. The exogenous thesis is silent: it makes no claim about when democracy arises | the exogenous thesis: democracy arose for another reason and did not survive without broad wealth |

The lecture left the last two rows open as an exercise. The entries are my own reading.

**The lesson of the cases:** what decides is *which* development takes place (broad industrialisation or income from raw materials) and whether the middle class is independent of the regime.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Lipset (1959)** states the link between economic development and democracy [@lipset1959some].
2. **Waves.** The lecture's chart of the share of democracies among all states shows three rises: slowly from about 1850 to the 1920s, from 1945 to the 1980s, and steeply after 1989. Between the 1920s and the mid-1940s the share fell by about half. The common name for this pattern is "waves of democratisation", each followed by a reverse wave. The name is from general knowledge, not from the slide.
3. **After 1989** the share rose to about half of all states. Fukuyama argued that liberal democracy had won for good [@fukuyama1992end].
4. **Przeworski and Limongi (1997)** separate the two readings and find support for the exogenous one [@przeworski1997modernization].
5. **Acemoglu et al. (2008)** show that the effect of income on democracy largely disappears when fixed differences between countries are controlled for [@acemoglu2008income].
6. **Today** the share of democracies has stopped rising, and research has turned to relapses (see *democratic backsliding* in [[Democratic State]]).

## Application

- **Forecasts.** The endogenous thesis predicts that a growing China will democratise. The exogenous thesis makes no such prediction. Which thesis one holds changes the expectation about the largest autocracy.
- **Foreign policy.** "Change through trade" rests on the endogenous thesis: economic ties create a middle class, which then demands democracy. If the thesis is weak, so is the policy.
- **State-building.** The exogenous thesis says that a democracy set up from outside after a war or a collapse survives only where the economy grows. That fits Germany and Japan after 1945 better than Afghanistan after 2001. This comparison is my own.
- **Raw materials.** Income from oil and gas makes a state rich without the broad industrialisation and the independent middle class that Lipset's chain needs.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Correlation is not causation.** Wealth and democracy may both follow from older causes such as institutions, colonial history or geography.
> - **Reverse causality.** Democracy may itself make countries richer, through legal certainty, stability and education.
> - **Few cases.** Regime changes are rare, and each has many possible causes. Statistical tests have little to work with.
> - **Western path as the norm.** "Modernisation" assumes that societies develop along one path towards the Western model (see [[Social Change]]).
> - **The threshold is not a guarantee.** "Hardly ever" is not "never", and the finding is drawn from the past.

## Key Connections

- [[Democratic State]]: the theory explains how the form of state described there comes about, and why it lasts.
- [[Autocratic State]]: a relapse leads back there. The three pillars of autocratic stability are the other side of the question why a regime does *not* democratise.
- [[Selectorate Theory]]: a ruler with a small winning coalition and income from raw materials has no reason to widen the coalition.
- [[Social Change]]: modernisation theory in sociology is the wider claim that societies develop along a similar path. The political version is one part of it.
- [[Value Change (Inglehart)]]: a second route from wealth to democracy. Material security shifts values towards self-expression and participation.
- [[Bias in Research]]: the debate between the two theses is a debate about confounding and reverse causality.
