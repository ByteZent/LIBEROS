> [!bluf]
> The policy cycle models policy-making as a sequence of stages: **agenda-setting → formulation → decision → implementation → evaluation**, with evaluation feeding back into the agenda. It is the most widely used way to *organise* the study of policy, but as a *theory* it is weak: real policy-making is messy, overlapping and political, and more causal frameworks were developed partly as a critique of it.

## Definition

The stages model goes back to Harold Lasswell's (1956) functional analysis of the decision process. Modern textbooks (Howlett, Ramesh & Perl) use five stages.

**In my words:** a map of *what has to happen* for a policy to exist, not a description of *how it happens*.

## Key Points

| Stage | Core question | Key concepts |
|---|---|---|
| Agenda-setting | Why does *this* problem get attention now? | Problem definition, focusing events, Kingdon's streams |
| Formulation | What options exist? | Policy instruments, advisory systems |
| Decision-making | Who chooses, and how? | Rationality vs incrementalism |
| Implementation | Does the policy do what it says? | Top-down vs bottom-up, street-level bureaucrats |
| Evaluation | Did it work, and for whom? | Outputs vs outcomes, policy learning |

## Application

Security and defence policy runs through the same stages: a threat perception reaches the **agenda**, a white paper or strategy is **formulated**, parliament **decides** on budget and procurement, the armed forces **implement**, and audits and after-action reviews **evaluate**. Compare [[Ends-Ways-Means]] on the military side.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Sabatier & Jenkins-Smith:** the stages model is not a causal theory. It has no drivers and no testable hypotheses, and stages overlap or are skipped.
> - **Lindblom (1959):** real decisions are incremental "muddling through", not rational stage-by-stage choice.
> - **Kingdon (1984):** problems, policies and politics flow independently and only couple when a *policy window* opens.
