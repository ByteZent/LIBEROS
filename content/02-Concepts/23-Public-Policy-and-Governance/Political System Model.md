> [!bluf]
> The model treats a state as a **system that turns inputs into outputs**. Citizens and groups send **demands and support** in. Inside, four **process functions** follow each other: interests are **articulated**, **aggregated**, turned into a **decision** and **implemented**. The **outputs** (laws, decisions, services) change society, and society answers with new demands. This is the **feedback** loop.
>
> The most useful thing it shows: between citizens and the state stand **intermediaries** (parties, interest groups, social movements, media). Whoever controls the early steps decides what reaches the decision at all.

## Question

How do the wishes of millions of people become decisions that bind all of them, and what happens after the decision?

## Assumptions

- **Politics can be drawn as a system** with a border. Inside is the political system, outside is its environment: society, the economy, other states, international and supranational institutions.
- **The same functions must be performed in every political system**, whatever its institutions look like. A democracy and a dictatorship both articulate and aggregate interests. They differ in *who* does it and *how*.
- **The state is the main frame of reference** for political action (see [[Sovereign Territorial State]]).

> [!tip] Test
> Drop the border. If decisions are made in Brussels, in a trade agreement or by a standard-setting industry body, inputs and outputs no longer belong to one state, and the model has to be drawn on several levels.

## Mechanism

A political system is the whole of the state and non-state institutions and actors, rules and procedures that take part, within a state, in formulating and solving political problems and in making and enforcing generally binding decisions [@bernauer2025einfuhrung, ch. 3.1].

Every political system has the same three building blocks:

- **Citizens:** the base of the system.
- **Political intermediaries:** parties, interest groups, social movements and media, which connect society with the decision-making system.
- **Central political decision-making system** (*zentrales politisches Entscheidungssystem*): legislature, executive and judiciary, where binding decisions are taken.

States differ in how these blocks are built and related. In democracies citizens have a much stronger input function and the three powers check each other. In autocracies power lies with a small elite or one person.

![[political-system-model.svg]]

1. **Inputs.** Demands (what people want), support (trust, taxes, obedience, votes) and opposition (for example demonstrations) enter the system. The decision-making system takes them up and turns them into binding decisions, or rejects them.
2. **Interest articulation.** A demand is voiced: by citizens, associations, social movements, the media.
3. **Interest aggregation.** Many single demands are bundled into a few options that can be decided on. This is the classic job of parties.
4. **Policy-making.** Government and parliament take a binding decision.
5. **Implementation.** The administration applies the decision. Courts settle disputes about it.
6. **Outputs and policy functions.** The outputs do three things to society: they **extract** resources (taxes, conscription), **regulate** behaviour (laws, bans) and **distribute** goods and services (pensions, schools, security).
7. **Feedback.** People experience the outputs and react. Their reaction is the next round of inputs.

### Political intermediaries

Intermediaries are the actors between citizens and the central decision-making institutions: parties, interest groups, social movements, communication platforms.

- **Transmission:** they carry interests and demands from the political base into the institutions that decide.
- **Steering of their own:** they also shape politics directly. Media coverage influences public opinion. Industry associations set up certification systems for global supply chains, which is rule-making without the state.

### The four steps in Switzerland

| Step | Who and how |
|---|---|
| Articulation | Associations and cantons in the consultation procedure (*Vernehmlassung*), popular initiatives, demonstrations, media |
| Aggregation | Parties and umbrella associations bundle demands; the Federal Council's draft is already a compromise |
| Policy-making | Federal Council and Federal Assembly; the people can decide last, through the referendum |
| Implementation | Federal administration and, to a large part, the cantons; courts |

## Formal Expression

The model has no equation. Its formal content is the diagram: **inputs → process functions → outputs → feedback → inputs**.

## Predictions & Evidence

The model is a **framework for description**, not a theory with testable hypotheses. It tells you where to look, not what you will find. Read as loose expectations:

| Expectation | Illustration | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Demands that find no channel look for another one | When parties do not take up an issue, it appears as a popular initiative or on the street | plausible, not a strict test |
| Outputs that disappoint lower support | Unpopular decisions cost trust and votes in the next round | plausible, not a strict test |
| Every system needs aggregation | Without parties or similar bodies, a parliament faces thousands of unsorted demands | plausible, not a strict test |

## Policy Implications

- **To explain a decision, start before the decision.** Ask who articulated the demand, who bundled it and what was filtered out on the way.
- **Reform can target a step.** Changing the electoral system changes aggregation. Changing the consultation procedure changes articulation. Neither touches the content of any law directly.
- **For analysis of a foreign state:** the four steps give a checklist for the "P" in [[PMESII]]. Who can voice demands? Who bundles them? Who decides? Who enforces? A regime in which the last three are the same small group reacts very differently from one where they are separate.
- **The input side is a point of attack.** Influence operations work on articulation and on support, long before any decision is taken (see [[Cognitive Warfare]]).

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Not falsifiable.** Almost any political event can be placed somewhere in the diagram. That makes it a map, not an explanation.
> - **Bias towards stability.** Systems models ask how a system keeps going. Conflict, breakdown and revolution appear only as disturbances.
> - **Demand-driven picture.** In the diagram politics starts with citizens. In practice governments, parties and media often create the demands they then respond to.
> - **The steps are not separate in reality.** Parliament articulates, aggregates and decides at once. The administration makes policy while implementing it.
> - **One state, one box.** Multi-level politics (cantons, federal state, EU, international organisations) and private rule-making fit badly.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Easton** introduced the political system as an input-output model with feedback: demands and support go in, authoritative decisions come out [@easton1965systems].
2. **Almond and Powell** opened the box and named the functions every system must perform: process functions (articulation, aggregation, policy-making, implementation and adjudication) and policy functions (extraction, regulation, distribution) [@almond1966comparative].
3. **Textbooks** combine both into one diagram, with the state inside an environment of other states and international institutions. The course textbook's diagram is based on Powell et al., *Comparative Politics Today* (2015), the successor of Almond and Powell's book [@bernauer2025einfuhrung].

## Key Connections

- [[Polity, Policy, Politics]]: the model puts the three in one picture. The box and its actors are *polity*, the sequence of steps is *politics*, the outputs are *policy*.
- [[Sovereign Territorial State]]: the box seen from outside. The other states in the environment are boxes of the same kind.
- [[Circular Flow Diagram]]: the same kind of model in economics. Both reduce a whole society to a few actors and flows that close into a loop.
- [[General Model Theory]]: a clear case of reduction for a purpose. The diagram leaves out almost everything in order to make the sequence visible.
- [[OODA Loop]]: another loop with feedback. The difference: Boyd's loop describes one actor deciding against an opponent, this one describes a society deciding for itself.

## Self-Test: Political System

> [!qard]- 1. Name the four process functions in order.
> Interest articulation → interest aggregation → policy-making → implementation.

> [!qard]- 2. What are inputs, outputs and feedback?
> Inputs are demands, support and opposition from society. Outputs are binding decisions, laws and services. Feedback is the reaction of society to the outputs, which becomes the next input.

> [!qard]- 3. What are the functions of political intermediaries? Give examples.
> They transmit interests and demands from citizens into the central decision-making institutions (parties, interest groups, social movements, communication platforms). They also steer on their own: media shape public opinion, industry associations run certification systems for supply chains.

> [!qard]- 4. What is the difference between articulation and aggregation?
> Articulation voices a single demand. Aggregation bundles many demands into a few options that can be decided on, for example a party programme.

> [!qard]- 5. Name the three policy functions.
> **Extraction** (taxes, conscription), **regulation** (laws, bans), **distribution** (pensions, schools, security).

> [!qard]- 6. Why is the model not a theory in the strict sense?
> It makes no statement that could turn out to be wrong. It sorts what happens into categories but does not say what will happen under which conditions.

> [!qard]- 7. Apply: the parties ignore an issue that many citizens care about. What does the model lead you to expect?
> The demand looks for another channel: a popular initiative, a social movement, the street. If the outputs keep disappointing, support falls in the next round (feedback).

> [!qard]- 8. Compare: a democracy and an autocracy in terms of the four process functions.
> Both must articulate, aggregate, decide and implement. They differ in who does it. In a democracy many actors take part and the powers check each other. In an autocracy the last three lie with the same small group.
