> [!bluf]
> Democracy means **rule by the people**. In a modern, representative democracy the people remain the sovereign and hand the exercise of power to representatives **in free and fair elections and for a limited time**, so that those who govern can be removed by the governed.
>
> That is the **narrow** definition: elections, competition and changes of power are enough. The **broad** definition adds further conditions, such as freedom of speech and association, the rule of law and separation of powers. Which one to use depends on the research question.
>
> Democracies differ a great deal among themselves: parliamentary or presidential, majoritarian or consensus, representative or direct. Switzerland is the standard example of a consensus democracy with strong direct-democratic rights.
>
> Democracy is a matter of **degree**, and the border with [[Autocratic State|autocracy]] is a grey zone, not a line.

## Definition

Democracy (Greek) means **rule by the people**. This rule has three dimensions [@bernauer2025einfuhrung]:

- it **comes from** the people;
- it is **exercised by** the people, or by representatives they elect;
- it is used **for the good of** the citizens.

Lincoln's formula of 1863 names the same three: "government of the people, by the people, and for the people".

The most important element is **popular sovereignty** (*Volkssouveränität*). Modern democracies are mostly **representative**: citizens no longer decide most issues themselves. The people remain the sovereign and the ultimate holder of state authority, and delegate its exercise to representatives.

For this delegation two features matter:

1. Power is transferred **directly, through free and fair elections**.
2. Power is transferred **for a limited time**. Office-holders must face re-election at regular intervals and stay only if the voters want them to.

From this follows the most important mark of a modern democracy: **political offices are filled by free and fair elections**. Such elections are **regular, general, equal, free, direct and secret**. They embody the two central dimensions of democracy [@dahl1971polyarchy]:

- **political competition** (contestation);
- **participation** of the citizens (inclusion).

### Narrow and broad definitions

Researchers agree that free and fair elections are the most important element and that democracy has several dimensions. They disagree about how many and which.

![[democracy-narrow-broad.svg]]

| Definition | A state is democratic if … | Authors |
|---|---|---|
| **Narrow** (*eng gefasst*, minimal) | … there are democratic elections, the political competition that goes with them, and changes of power | Schumpeter, Przeworski, Alvarez et al. [@schumpeter1942capitalism] |
| **Broad** (*umfassend*) | … further conditions are met in addition to democratic elections | Dahl, Diamond, Ferrín and Kriesi [@dahl1971polyarchy] |

- **Schumpeter's narrow definition:** democracy is the institutional arrangement in which individuals gain the power to decide through a competitive struggle for the people's vote.
- **Dahl's additions:** freedom of speech and of the press, alternative sources of information, and freedom of association (parties, interest groups).
- **Further criteria in broad definitions:** social and economic justice, effective separation of powers, the rule of law, civilian control of the military, gender equality, protection of minorities, human rights for everyone living in the country, and strong participation of citizens in decisions.
- **The caveat:** for these further criteria it is open whether they are core elements of democracy or rather its consequences and side effects.
- **Which one to use:** a definition must fit the theoretical argument. Depending on the research question a narrow or a broad one can make sense, as long as the reasoning and the data collection are transparent.

In my own words: in a democracy the losers of an election accept the result because they can win the next one, and the winners cannot use their power to make sure there is no next one.

> [!info] Beyond the textbook
> Merkel's **embedded democracy** is a well-known broad definition that the textbook's definition section does not use: elections are embedded in further "partial regimes" (political rights, civil rights, horizontal accountability between the powers, and the effective power of the elected to govern). A regime with free elections in which one of these is impaired is a **defective democracy** [@merkel2004embedded].

## Key Points

- **Elections are necessary but not sufficient.** Many autocracies hold elections. What counts is whether they are free, fair and can be lost.
- **What elections do.** They let citizens vote out rulers who do not act in their interest, settle conflicts peacefully, and discipline governments: one that wants to be re-elected cannot stray far from what a majority wants for long.
- **Two dimensions:** who may take part (participation) and whether there is a real choice (competition). A state can be high on one and low on the other.
- **Democracy has to be defined before it can be measured.** One simple operationalisation uses four indicators: Is the legislature elected? Is the executive elected? Is there more than one party? Has power changed hands peacefully? (See [[Research Design]] on concept, dimensions and indicators.)
- **A broader definition gives fewer democracies.** Under the narrow definition a state with fair elections and weak courts counts as a democracy. Under a broad one it does not, or only as a **defective democracy**.

### Types of democracy

Democracies limit state power by three principles: free and fair elections, the **rule of law**, and the **separation of powers** [@bernauer2025einfuhrung]. They differ in how they separate powers:

- **Horizontal separation:** between legislature, executive and judiciary. The relation of legislature and executive gives the three systems of government.
- **Vertical separation:** between the national level and the levels below it (cantons, *Länder*) or above it (the EU).

**Systems of government** differ on two features: how the government is elected, and whether it depends on the confidence of parliament.

![[democracy-systems-of-government.svg]]

| System | Election of the executive | Removal | Powers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Parliamentary** | Indirect: only parliament is elected by the people, and the government comes out of parliament | Parliament can bring the government down by a vote of no confidence | Fused: government and parliamentary majority depend on each other | United Kingdom, Germany |
| **Presidential** | The president is elected by the people, separately from parliament | Fixed terms: parliament can remove the president only in extreme cases, and the president cannot dissolve parliament | Clearly separated | United States |
| **Semi-presidential** | A president elected by the people, and a prime minister with a cabinet | The cabinet is responsible to parliament | President and prime minister share executive power | France (strong president), Austria (weak president) |

**Further distinctions**

| Distinction | Variants | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Vertical separation of powers** | **Unitary state:** the main decisions are taken at the national level.<br />**Federal state:** national and regional governments each have areas in which they decide finally, guaranteed by the constitution. Typical features: a second chamber, a written constitution, strong constitutional review | United Kingdom · Switzerland, Germany, United States |
| **Overall pattern** | **Majoritarian:** the majority governs alone, power concentrated.<br />**Consensus:** power shared among many parties and levels | United Kingdom · Switzerland [@lijphart1999patterns] |
| **Who decides on issues** | **Representative:** elected representatives decide.<br />**Direct:** citizens decide on issues themselves.<br />**Semi-direct** (*halbdirekt*): a combination of both | Most democracies · ancient Athens · Switzerland |

- **Lijphart's finding:** consensus democracies do no worse economically than majoritarian ones and do better on corruption, gender equality, social and environmental policy and participation. The approach has been criticised as too static.
- **Veto players** (Tsebelis): a second way to look at the whole system with one variable. The more veto players have to agree, and the further apart their preferences are, the harder reform becomes and the more stable policy is.

**Switzerland** fits the typology of systems of government only to a limited extent:

- **Election:** both chambers together (the United Federal Assembly) elect the Federal Council, as in a parliamentary system.
- **No removal:** parliament cannot force the Federal Council to resign during the term, and the Federal Council cannot dissolve parliament. In this the relation resembles a **presidential** system.
- **Collegial government:** seven members with equal rights. There is no separate office of president: one of the seven holds it for a year, with mainly representative duties.
- **Magic formula** (*Zauberformel*): the four largest parties share the seats according to their strength over the last decades, so that elections have no direct effect on the composition of the government.
- **Direct democracy:** very strong rights of the citizens to decide on issues in frequent popular votes.

Outside the textbook this arrangement is usually called a **directorial system**. Together with federalism, concordance (all large parties govern together) and direct democracy, it makes Switzerland a **semi-direct consensus democracy**.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Athens** (5th century BC): direct rule by the assembly of citizens, a small part of the population.
2. **Representative government** (17th to 18th century): the English, American and French revolutions bind rulers to law and to elected assemblies.
3. **Extension of the vote** (19th to 20th century): step by step to all men, then to women. Switzerland introduced women's suffrage at the federal level only in 1971.
4. **Waves:** the number of democracies grew in waves (after 1918, after 1945, after 1974 and 1989), each followed by a reverse wave.
5. **Today:** the debate is about **democratic backsliding**: elected governments that weaken courts, media and electoral rules from the inside.

## Application

**Why regime type matters for security.**

- **Democratic peace:** democracies fight wars, but very rarely against each other. This is one of the best-supported regularities in international relations, although the reason is disputed [@doyle1983kant].
- **Democracies and war:** there is evidence that democracies win a large share of the wars they start. Proposed reasons are that they choose their wars more carefully, because leaders can be voted out, and that their soldiers show more initiative [@reiter2002democracies]. The finding is contested.
- **Civilian control:** in a democracy the armed forces are subordinate to elected authorities. In Switzerland this goes far: parliament elects the general only in case of war, and the militia principle ties the army to the citizenry [@bv1999art58].
- **Open societies are open targets.** Free media and open debate are what make a democracy work, and they are also the entry points for foreign influence (see [[Cognitive Warfare]]).

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **The narrow definition is too thin.** It counts states as democratic that hold fair elections but do not protect minorities or limit the government between elections.
> - **The broad definition mixes things.** If rule of law, civil rights and good governance are all part of "democracy", the concept can no longer be used to ask whether democracy *causes* them.
> - **Western template.** Most indices measure closeness to the liberal democracies of Western Europe and North America.
> - **Majority rule is not the same as good outcomes.** A majority can decide to restrict the rights of a minority. Democratic theory needs something outside majority rule (constitution, courts, international law) to deal with this, and that something is itself not democratic.
> - **Indices disagree at the margins.** The same country can be a democracy in one index and a hybrid regime in another. For cases near the border, the classification is a choice of the researcher.

## Self-Test: Democracy

> [!qard]- 1. What does democracy mean, and which three dimensions does it have?
> Rule by the people. It comes from the people, is exercised by the people or their elected representatives, and is used for the good of the citizens.

> [!qard]- 2. Which two features make delegation to representatives democratic?
> Power is transferred directly through free and fair elections, and only for a limited time, so that office-holders must face re-election.

> [!qard]- 3. Name the six attributes of democratic elections.
> Regular, general, equal, free, direct, secret.

> [!qard]- 4. Name Dahl's two dimensions.
> **Political competition** (contestation) and **participation** of the citizens (inclusion).

> [!qard]- 5. What separates the narrow from the broad definition of democracy?
> - **Narrow:** democratic elections, political competition and changes of power are enough (Schumpeter, Przeworski).
> - **Broad:** further conditions are required, for example freedom of speech, press and association (Dahl), the rule of law, separation of powers, protection of minorities.
> - **Caveat:** the further criteria may be consequences of democracy and not part of it.

> [!qard]- 6. Parliamentary or presidential: what is the key difference?
> Two features: how the government is elected, and whether it depends on parliament's confidence.
> - **Parliamentary:** the government comes out of parliament and can be brought down by a vote of no confidence.
> - **Presidential:** the president is elected by the people for a fixed term, cannot be removed by parliament except in extreme cases, and cannot dissolve it.

> [!qard]- 7. What is a semi-presidential system?
> A president elected by the people and a prime minister whose cabinet is responsible to parliament share executive power. Example: France.

> [!qard]- 8. Why does Switzerland fit neither the parliamentary nor the presidential type?
> Parliament elects the Federal Council (parliamentary), but cannot remove it during the term, and the Federal Council cannot dissolve parliament (presidential).

> [!qard]- 9. Why is Switzerland called a consensus democracy?
> Power is shared widely: all large parties sit in government (concordance), the cantons hold strong competences (federalism), and the people can overrule parliament (referendum, initiative).

> [!qard]- 10. What does the democratic peace claim, and what does it not claim?
> That democracies very rarely go to war against each other. It does not claim that democracies are peaceful in general.

> [!qard]- 11. Apply: a state holds fair, competitive elections, but its courts are weak and minorities are unprotected. Is it a democracy?
> Under the narrow definition yes. Under a broad one no, or only a *defective democracy*. The answer depends on the definition, which has to fit the research question and be stated openly.

> [!qard]- 12. Judge: what is lost when rule of law and good governance are made part of the definition of democracy?
> You can no longer ask whether democracy *causes* them, because cause and effect sit in the same concept. A broader definition also leaves fewer states that count as democracies.
