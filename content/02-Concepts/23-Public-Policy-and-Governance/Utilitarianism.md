> [!bluf]
> Utilitarianism judges every law and institution by one test: does it lead to the **greatest happiness of the greatest number**? Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) built it on the assumption that people are alike, self-interested and able to calculate consequences. Government then has one task: to reward socially useful actions and punish harmful ones by fixed, known rules.
>
> For History I it matters as one of the ruling ideologies of early 19th-century England. It gave reformers a tool against inherited privilege and judge-made law, and with the panopticon a model for prisons, workhouses and schools. But it was less dominant than its fame suggests: the ruling outlook was religious, and Paley was more representative than Bentham.
>
> Its weak points are that it ignores character and cannot rank pleasures, and that its minimal state did not stay minimal.

## Definition

The principle of utility: that which leads to the "greatest happiness of the greatest number" is the best criterion by which to judge inherited knowledge and rules [@hilton2006mad, p. 331].

**In my words:** do not ask whether a rule is old, natural or God-given. Ask what it does to the sum of happiness. Whatever cannot pass that test is superstition or privilege.

Bentham's version rests on two notions [@hilton2006mad, p. 330]:

1. "Self-regarding affections" are stronger than social ones.
2. Individuals weigh the consequences of their actions and choose what promises the most happiness. This weighing is the **felicific calculus**.

## Key Points

- **No natural rights.** Bentham called them "nonsense on stilts". Rights are what the law grants because it is useful [@hilton2006mad, p. 330].
- **The world as a machine.** Individuals are machines too and should be left to make their own choices. Wrong choices hurt, and the pain teaches. The magistrate should not tell people what to think: he lays down rules, names the penalties and punishes. Bentham "virtually reduced governance to jurisprudence" [@hilton2006mad, p. 313].
- **Rule instead of discretion.** Bentham wanted a "pannomion", a complete body of fixed legislation, in place of the common law and its judge-made precedents. Sentencing should be scientific: a correct proportion between offence and punishment [@hilton2006mad, p. 319, 328].
- **Government in a "tutelary role".** It rewards good (socially motivated) actions and punishes bad ones. Discretionary rule by princes, priests, lawyers or parish overseers would end, because even with good intentions it rests on subjective ideas of right behaviour [@hilton2006mad, p. 331].
- **A tool, not a dogma.** Hilton reads it as a tool of analysis more than an a priori theory of behaviour. Bentham knew that most knowledge is handed down: it only has to be questioned constantly [@hilton2006mad, p. 331].
- **Politically radical from 1809–10.** Republicanism, annual elections, the secret ballot, and universal male and female suffrage subject to a literacy test. Bentham sided with the underdog and did not share the fear of revolution of his contemporaries [@hilton2006mad, p. 330].

### The panopticon

A circular prison with the cells on the outside and the inspector at the centre, who sees without being seen. Not every inmate can be watched at every moment, but each should "conceive himself" to be watched. Constant good behaviour becomes a habit of mind. Bentham thought of it as humane and as a way to rehabilitation, and meant the principle for factories, workhouses, madhouses, hospitals and schools as well [@hilton2006mad, p. 329].

## Origins & Evolution

- **Bentham** had legal training but never practised, and called lawyers "the scum of the earth". He was cold-shouldered by most of the great, apart from his patron Lord Shelburne (Lansdowne), and almost unknown for most of his life [@hilton2006mad, p. 328].
- **1791–1813:** the panopticon absorbs him. The Government rejects it in 1813 in favour of the evangelical penitentiary. The frustration may explain his turn to political radicalism [@hilton2006mad, p. 329–330].
- **1820s:** fame through disciples. Dumont (translation), Romilly (law), James Mill (philosophy), Francis Place (practical politics), the *Westminster Review* (1824), London University (1828) and, after 1832, the "Philosophic Radicals" in Parliament [@hilton2006mad, p. 328].
- **1829:** Macaulay's attack in the *Edinburgh Review* on their deductive method and their blindness to beauty, history and feeling [@hilton2006mad, p. 348].
- **John Stuart Mill** repairs the doctrine: utility must be "grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being", and it is "better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied" [@hilton2006mad, p. 331–332]. *On Liberty* (1859) defends liberty on utilitarian grounds, not by natural rights [@evans2011shaping, p. 263].

## Application

How it shows in the England of the course:

| Field | What Utilitarians wanted | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Criminal law | Lenient but fixed penalties, no judicial discretion | Peel reforms in the same direction of fixity, but harshly [@hilton2006mad, p. 320] |
| Prisons | The panopticon | Rejected in 1813. The regime of the penitentiary was not so different in practice [@hilton2006mad, p. 332] |
| Constitution | Universal suffrage, secret ballot, annual elections | Not adopted. Whigs such as Mackintosh feared a tyranny of the majority [@hilton2006mad, p. 348] |
| Education | Secular higher education | London University, 1828 |

**Allies it should not have had.** Utilitarians and evangelicals worked together on penal reform, prison reform, anti-slavery and temperance, although James Mill denied God and Bentham ignored him. The reason: both saw the world as causes and consequences, both saw people as flawed but improvable, and the evangelical God ruled "by reward and punishment" like Bentham's magistrate. The world was a "cosmic panopticon" [@hilton2006mad, p. 332–333].

**Liberal Tories** thought in the same mechanical way and are, in Mill's distinction, the "Benthamites" among the Tories. But they lacked his optimism: Bentham expected individual choice to produce the greatest happiness, Peel wanted to teach "moral discipline" [@hilton2006mad, p. 314, 327]. See [[Liberal Toryism vs High Toryism]].

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **No account of character.** If everyone is psychologically the same, only disposition counts. So Bentham held that a crime under strong temptation needs a *harsher* penalty, to offset the temptation. That is logical, but it ignores that giving in to a slight temptation is more depraved [@hilton2006mad, p. 331].
> - **No ranking of pleasures.** Only the amount of pleasure counts, so pushpin is as good as poetry. It needed the younger Mill to answer this [@hilton2006mad, p. 332].
> - **No sense of difference.** James Mill's *History of British India* shows little sense of racial or gender difference, or of any difference at all [@hilton2006mad, p. 330].
> - **The minimal state does not stay minimal.** A free market needs a strong state to police it. Bureaucrats are blamed for bad outcomes, so they intervene, and by their own ideas of fairness. Through this "bureaucratic creep" Bentham's "night watchman" state slides towards the paternalist state of the later 19th century [@hilton2006mad, p. 332].
> - **Surveillance.** Because of the panopticon Bentham is often seen as reinforcing the subordination of the poor. Hilton calls this a myth as to his intentions: Bentham saw himself attacking legal privilege [@hilton2006mad, p. 330].
> - **Less influential than assumed.** The "machine philosophy" of the age came from Newtonian science and evangelical religion as much as from Bentham [@hilton2006mad, p. 313].

## Key Connections

- [[Liberal Toryism vs High Toryism]]: liberal Tories share the Utilitarians' mechanical view of society and their preference for fixed rules over discretion, but ground it in religion and expect suffering, not happiness.
- [[Britain 1780–1939 (Timeline)]]: it places the panopticon (1791), the *Westminster Review* (1824) and London University (1828) among the other ideologies of the period.
- [[Consumer Choice]]: modern microeconomics keeps Bentham's picture of the individual who weighs alternatives and maximises utility, without his claim to measure happiness across persons.
- [[Democratic State]]: Bentham's later programme (universal suffrage, secret ballot, frequent elections) derives democracy from utility instead of from natural rights.

## Self-Test: Utilitarianism

> [!qard]- 1. Recall: what is the principle of utility?
> Whatever leads to the greatest happiness of the greatest number is the criterion by which laws, institutions and inherited rules are to be judged.

> [!qard]- 2. Recall: on which two assumptions about people did Bentham build?
> Self-regarding affections outweigh social ones, and individuals weigh the consequences of their actions and choose the greatest happiness (felicific calculus).

> [!qard]- 3. Explain: why did Bentham reject natural rights and the common law?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> Neither passes the test of utility. Natural rights are "nonsense on stilts", and judge-made precedent means discretion and privilege for lawyers. He wanted a complete code of fixed legislation.

> [!qard]- 4. Explain: how was the panopticon meant to reform the inmate?
> The inmate cannot see the inspector and must assume he is always watched. Constant good behaviour turns into a habit of mind. Bentham thought this humane and rehabilitating.

> [!qard]- 5. Compare: why could Utilitarians and evangelicals work together although the former ignored God?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> <!-- qard-important -->
> Both saw the world as causes and consequences and people as flawed but improvable. The evangelical God governs by reward and punishment, as Bentham's magistrate should.

> [!qard]- 6. Apply: a thief stole under strong temptation. What sentence follows from Bentham's logic, and what does this show?
> A harsher one, to offset the temptation. It shows the weak point: the doctrine has no place for character, since giving in to a slight temptation is more depraved.

> [!qard]- 7. Limit: why did Bentham's "night watchman" state not stay minimal?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> A free market needs the state to police it. Officials are blamed for bad outcomes and intervene by their own ideas of fairness: "bureaucratic creep" towards a paternalist state.

> [!qard]- 8. Judge: was Bentham the leading thinker of his age?
> Hilton says no. The mechanical outlook came from Newtonian science, natural theology and evangelical religion. Paley was more representative, and Bentham became famous only in the 1820s.
