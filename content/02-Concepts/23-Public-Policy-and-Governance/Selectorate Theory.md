> [!bluf]
> Selectorate theory explains what rulers do from one aim: **staying in power**. It needs two numbers. The **selectorate (S)** is everyone with a say in who rules. The **winning coalition (W)** is the part of S whose support the ruler needs.
>
> Supporters must be rewarded. **Private goods** (offices, licences, money) cost more with every person who gets them. **Public goods** (schools, roads, clean air) cost the same however many benefit. So a ruler with a small W buys loyalty with private goods, and a ruler with a large W can only afford public goods.
>
> The point of the theory: **the size of W decides, not whether the ruler is good or bad.** Democracies have a large W, so they provide more public goods.

## Definition

The theory predicts policy from the number of people who may take part in choosing the ruler (the selectorate, S) and the number whose support a ruler needs to keep power (the winning coalition, W) [@buenodemesquita2003logic].

| | Selectorate (S) | Winning coalition (W) |
|---|---|---|
| **Democracy** | Everyone entitled to vote | The majority of voters needed to win |
| **Autocracy** | Oligarchs, officers, party cadres, the ruler's family | The few whose support is enough: in a military regime, the officers needed for the coup and to hold power |

**In my words:** a ruler is an employer who must pay the people who keep him in office. With a hundred of them he pays each one. With two million he cannot, so he builds something all of them can use.

## Key Points

### Two kinds of goods

| | Private goods | Public goods |
|---|---|---|
| **Properties** | Excludable and rival | Not excludable, not rival |
| **Examples** | Lucrative offices, licences to extract raw materials, money | Legal certainty, a stable currency, low crime, schools, roads, clean air |
| **Cost** | Grows with every further person | Fixed, whatever the number of users |

### The ruler's arithmetic

The lecture's example: every member of W must receive benefits worth CHF 2,000, or they change to a rival. Public goods cost a fixed CHF 1 billion.

| Regime | W | Cost of private goods | Cost of public goods | Cheaper |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Military junta | 100 | CHF 200,000 | CHF 1 billion | private |
| One-party regime | 10,000 | CHF 20 million | CHF 1 billion | private |
| Democracy | 2 million | CHF 4 billion | CHF 1 billion | **public** |

With these numbers the two costs are equal at W = 500,000 (1 billion ÷ 2,000). Below that, private goods are the cheaper way to stay in power. Above it, public goods are. The break-even point is my own calculation from the lecture's figures.

**The same logic seen from a supporter.** Suppose the ruler has CHF 1 billion to hand out as private goods. Split among a junta of 100 that is CHF 10 million each. Split among 2 million voters it is CHF 500 each. The larger W, the less a private share is worth, and the more a member gains from a school or a road. This second calculation is mine.

### Loyalty

What counts is the **ratio of W to S**: the chance that a member of today's coalition will also belong to the next one.

- **Small W in a large S** (a personalist autocracy): loyalty is very high. A member of the coalition who is dropped has little chance of belonging to the next one, so members cling to the ruler.
- **Large W relative to S** (a democracy): loyalty is low. A voter is likely to be in the winning coalition after a change of government too, so the government must follow the coalition's wishes closely.

| | W | S | Loyalty of the coalition | Typical means |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Democracy** | large | large | low | public goods |
| **Personalist autocracy** | small | large | very high | private goods, cheaply |
| **Junta, monarchy** | small | small | lower: members are hard to replace | private goods, at a higher price per member |

The first two rows are from the textbook. The third follows from the same ratio and is my own completion.

### What follows

- **Distribution of wealth.** A large W is paid with goods that everyone uses, so wealth spreads. A small W receives private goods: a small elite grows rich while the country grows poor.
- **A comparison, not a law.** The claim holds as a tendency and in comparison. Autocracies provide some public goods and democracies hand out private ones.
- **Preferences can be the same.** Unlike the [[Median Voter Theorem]], the theory does not need the elite to want something different from the people. The difference comes from cost alone. The two explanations are set side by side in [[Democratic State#Does democracy solve problems better?]].
- **It also works inside democracies** (ch. 7). The more parties a system has, the smaller the share of the vote a party needs for a role in parliament or government: a smaller winning coalition. A governing party in a two-party system needs over 50 % and reaches them best with public goods. In a multi-party system parties serve one social group with targeted benefits. So more fragmented party systems produce fewer public goods.

## Origins & Evolution

- Bueno de Mesquita, Smith, Siverson and Morrow set out the theory in *The Logic of Political Survival* [@buenodemesquita2003logic].
- It belongs to the rational-choice tradition: rulers are treated as actors who maximise their time in office, whatever their convictions.
- The textbook presents it in ch. 3 as the second of two explanations for the different problem-solving capacity of democracies and autocracies [@bernauer2025einfuhrung].

## Application

- **Sanctions.** To change an autocrat's behaviour, hit the winning coalition: the generals, party leaders and oligarchs he pays with private goods. Sanctions that hit the population may not reach him.
- **Reading a regime.** Estimate W. A junta, a party regime and a personal dictatorship differ in the size of the group that must be paid, and so in how much they spend on the population (see [[Autocratic State]]).
- **Foreign aid and resource income.** Money that reaches the ruler without taxing the population can be turned into private goods for W. The theory predicts that such income stabilises small-coalition regimes. This is the theory's claim as I understand it, not material from the course.
- **Armies.** In a small-coalition regime the officers are part of W. That gives a reason for promotion by loyalty and for the coup-proofing described in [[Autocratic State]].

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **W and S are hard to measure.** Who exactly belongs to the winning coalition of China or Saudi Arabia? Without independent measures the theory risks reading W off the outcome it is meant to explain.
> - **Only one motive.** Rulers also act from ideology, nationalism or religion. The theory treats these as noise.
> - **Public goods in autocracies.** Some autocracies have built schools, roads and health systems on a large scale. A small W does not forbid public goods where they also serve the ruler, for example growth that pays the coalition.
> - **Two numbers for very different regimes.** The theory puts every regime on the same two scales and ignores institutions such as parties, courts and federalism.
> - **The lecture's example simplifies.** It fixes the price of a supporter at CHF 2,000 for every regime. In the theory the price itself depends on loyalty: a member of a small coalition in a large selectorate can be had for less.
> - The first two points are standard objections. The examples and the last point are my own.

## Key Connections

- [[Median Voter Theorem]]: the other explanation of the same finding. It rests on different preferences, selectorate theory on different costs.
- [[Autocratic State]]: a small winning coalition is what the regime types have in common. Co-optation, one of the three pillars of stability, is the handing out of private goods.
- [[Democratic State]]: a large winning coalition is the reason elections lead to public goods.
- [[Ten Principles of Economics]]: rulers respond to incentives. The theory is that principle applied to political survival.
- [[PMESII]]: the winning coalition is the core of the political factor when the opponent is an autocracy.

## Self-Test: Selectorate

> [!qard]- 1. Define selectorate and winning coalition.
> The selectorate (S) is everyone with a say in who rules. The winning coalition (W) is the part of S whose support the ruler needs to stay in power.

> [!qard]- 2. What separates public from private goods, and how do their costs behave?
> Public goods are not excludable and not rival: their cost is fixed. Private goods are excludable and rival: their cost grows with every person who receives them.

> [!qard]- 3. Why does a large winning coalition lead to public goods?
> Rewarding every member with private goods becomes too dear. Public goods cost the same however many benefit, so they are the cheaper way to keep a large coalition.

> [!qard]- 4. Calculate: each member of W must receive CHF 2,000, public goods cost CHF 1 billion. At what size of W are the two equally dear?
> W = 500,000.
> <!-- qard-solution -->
> 1. Cost of private goods: W × 2,000.
> 2. Set equal to 1,000,000,000: W = 1,000,000,000 ÷ 2,000 = 500,000.
> 3. Below it private goods are cheaper, above it public goods.

> [!qard]- 5. Calculate: with the same figures, which is cheaper for a junta with W = 100 and for a democracy with W = 2 million?
> Junta: private goods (CHF 200,000). Democracy: public goods (CHF 1 billion against CHF 4 billion).
> <!-- qard-solution -->
> 1. Junta: 100 × 2,000 = CHF 200,000, far below CHF 1 billion.
> 2. Democracy: 2,000,000 × 2,000 = CHF 4 billion, above CHF 1 billion.
> 3. Private goods grow with W, public goods cost the same. The size of W decides.

> [!qard]- 6. Diagram: name the two lines (1) and (2) and the point (3).
> ![[selectorate-theory-winning-coalition.svg]]
> <!-- qard-hide: private goods: W × CHF 2,000; public goods: fixed, CHF 1 bn; W = 500,000 -->
> (1) The cost of private goods, which rises with W. (2) The fixed cost of public goods. (3) The size of W at which both cost the same.

> [!qard]- 7. Why is loyalty highest in a personalist autocracy?
> W is small and S is large. A member who is dropped is unlikely to belong to the next coalition, so members hold to the ruler.

> [!qard]- 8. Apply: sanctions are meant to change an autocrat's behaviour. Whom must they hit?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> The small winning coalition whose support keeps him in power and whom he pays with private goods. Sanctions on the population may not reach him.

> [!qard]- 9. Apply: why do more fragmented party systems produce fewer public goods, according to the theory?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> With more parties, a party needs a smaller share of the vote to matter: a smaller winning coalition. It serves one social group with targeted benefits. A party that needs over 50 % reaches them best with public goods.

> [!qard]- 10. Compare: selectorate theory and the median voter theorem.
> Both predict more public goods in democracies. Median voter: the preferences of those who decide differ. Selectorate: a large winning coalition is cheaper to satisfy with public goods, even if preferences are the same.

> [!qard]- 11. Limit: name one weakness of the theory.
> W and S are hard to measure independently of the outcome, and the theory knows only one motive: staying in power.
