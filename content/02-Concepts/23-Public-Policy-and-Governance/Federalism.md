> [!bluf]
> Federalism is the **vertical separation of powers**: state authority is divided between the national level and the levels below it, and each decides finally in some areas. In a **unitary state** the main decisions lie with the national level.
>
> Federal states usually have three features that reduce tension between the levels: a **second chamber**, a **written constitution** and **strong constitutional review**.
>
> Federalism keeps diversity, fits policy to regions and limits the concentration of power. It pays for this with slowness, less transparency and unclear responsibility. In a crisis federal states tend to centralise, as Switzerland and Germany did in the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Definition

**Vertical separation of powers:** the division of state authority between the national and the subnational levels of a state. Systems are **centralised** (unitary) or **federal**.

**Federal:** in certain policy fields decisions are taken and carried out at the subnational level as well. The subnational units hold these powers in their own right [@bernauer2025einfuhrung].

Three forms lie on one scale, from most to least central:

| Form | Who decides finally | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Unitary state** (*Einheitsstaat*) | The national level. Regions carry out national policy, and their powers can be taken back by an ordinary law | United Kingdom |
| **Federal state** (*Bundesstaat*) | Each level in its own areas, fixed in the constitution | Switzerland, Germany, United States |
| **Confederation** (*Staatenbund*) | The member states. They stay sovereign and share a few institutions | The EU comes closer to this than to a federal state |

**In my words:** in a federal state a canton does some things because it has the right to, not because the capital allows it. The constitution says which things, and the capital cannot change that alone.

## Key Points

### Three typical features

| Feature | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Second chamber** | The member states are represented in national law-making |
| **Written constitution** | Fixes the division of powers between the levels |
| **Strong constitutional review** | A court settles disputes about who may decide what |

- **The features serve one purpose:** they reduce possible tension between the nation state and its subnational units.
- **Every level has its own three powers.** Because the subnational units decide alone in some fields, they need their own democratically elected executive, legislature and judiciary. Exception: in Austria the judiciary is entirely federal.
- **Switzerland is untypical in the third feature.** The Federal Supreme Court has fewer powers than the constitutional courts of Germany and Austria. The textbook gives the strong direct democracy as background. That the people and not a court should have the last word is my own conclusion.
- **Subsidiarity:** every power to decide should lie at the lowest possible political level.

### Switzerland

Not from the lecture: this is general knowledge about the Federal Constitution, to be checked against the text.

- **Three levels:** Confederation, 26 cantons, communes.
- **The cantons hold the residual power.** The Confederation has only the powers the Federal Constitution gives it. Everything else stays with the cantons (Art. 3).
- **The three features in Swiss form:**
    - Second chamber: the Council of States, with two seats for each canton and one for each of the six former half-cantons.
    - Written constitution: a new federal power needs a change of the constitution, and that needs a majority of the people **and** of the cantons (*Ständemehr*).
    - Constitutional review: weak. The Federal Supreme Court must apply federal acts even if they contradict the constitution (Art. 190). It can review cantonal law.
- **Executive federalism** (*Vollzugsföderalismus*): the cantons carry out most federal law, so they shape policy even where the Confederation legislates.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Keeps diversity.** It integrates states that are mixed in ethnicity, religion or language | **More actors and more interests** raise the need for compromise and time: the system is slow to react and to reform |
| **Policy differs by region,** which protects minorities that live concentrated in one area | **State activity is harder to follow,** even opaque, which lowers participation |
| **More efficient,** because member states fit policy to their own needs. This holds above all when they **compete** and decide by **subsidiarity** | **Responsibility is less clear:** it is harder to say who answers for a decision |
| **Less concentration of state power** | |
| **More chances to take part,** which raises democratic legitimacy | |

The list of advantages is the theory. Empirical research finds fewer positive effects than was once expected.

### How strong is federalism?

- Among Germany, Austria and Switzerland it is strongest in **Switzerland**: the cantons have their own constitutions and courts and wide powers, for example in social assistance and large parts of health and education. Austria's *Länder* have few.
- Possible measures: the share of the subnational levels in public spending and tax revenue (fiscal decentralisation), the number of policy fields they control, and their freedom to set taxes. These measures are my own suggestions.
- The **EU** can be read as quasi-federal. The main feature is met: powers are clearly divided between the Union and the member states. The Union decides much in foreign trade, the states keep internal security and foreign policy. Of the three typical features it has only the strong court (the Court of Justice). The textbook counts no second chamber and no constitution, only treaties that work like one. The vertical division is so strong that the EU resembles a confederation more than a federal state.

### Case: federalism in the COVID-19 pandemic

In Switzerland and Germany the cantons and *Länder* decided much themselves.

| Advantages in practice | Disadvantages in practice |
|---|---|
| **Regional fit:** Ticino reacted in March 2020 before the Confederation, because it lies next to Lombardy | **A patchwork:** neighbouring cantons and *Länder* had different rules, which were hard to understand |
| **Experimenting and learning:** different measures could be compared | **Unclear responsibility:** in the second wave of autumn 2020 Confederation and cantons waited for each other |
| **Closeness to the people:** cantons know their hospitals, schools and local situation | **Slow coordination:** in Germany the rounds of federal and *Länder* governments took long to agree |

**Reaction: centralisation in the crisis.** Switzerland declared the "extraordinary situation" (March to June 2020), in which the Confederation decides. Germany passed the *Bundesnotbremse* (April 2021) with uniform national rules.

**What the case shows:** every advantage in the table has its disadvantage as a twin. Regional fit is the same thing as a patchwork, seen from the other side. Which one weighs more depends on how far the problem crosses borders. A virus does, a school timetable does not. This reading is mine.

## Origins & Evolution

This section is from general knowledge, not from the course.

1. **United States, 1787:** the constitution creates the first modern federal state, as a compromise between a loose league of states and a central government.
2. **Switzerland, 1848:** after the Sonderbund War the confederation of cantons becomes a federal state. The Council of States is modelled on the US Senate.
3. **Germany, 1949:** the Basic Law restores federalism, partly to prevent a new concentration of power.
4. **Today:** many unitary states have passed powers downwards (the United Kingdom to Scotland and Wales, Spain to its regions) without becoming federal states.

## Application

- **Analysing a reform.** Ask at which level the power lies and who must agree. In Switzerland a constitutional change needs a majority of the people *and* of the cantons, so the cantons are a veto player (see [[Democratic State]]).
- **Security and crisis management.** Police is cantonal, the army federal. A crisis that crosses cantonal borders raises the pandemic's question again: who decides, and when does the Confederation take over? For epidemics the law gives a graded answer in three steps: normal, particular and extraordinary situation, with more power for the Confederation at each step.
- **Armed forces.** Subsidiarity is also the rule for the army's support of civilian authorities: the army acts only when civilian means are not enough, and the civilian authority stays in charge. [[Mission Command]] is the same idea inside one organisation: decide at the lowest level that can.
- **Budget discipline.** One hypothesis says federal systems run smaller deficits, because the lowest level knows needs best and competition disciplines. The counter-argument: member states enjoy the benefits of spending and pass the costs on to the whole state, above all when they live on transfers and not on their own taxes.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Efficiency is conditional.** The gain appears only where member states compete and pay for their own decisions. Where they are financed by transfers, the incentive runs the other way.
> - **Unequal conditions.** Regional differences in taxes, schools and services can be read as diversity or as inequality between citizens of the same state.
> - **Slowness can be the point.** What looks like a weakness is, from the view of limiting power, the purpose. The same feature is listed on both sides of the table.
> - **Crises centralise.** If a federal state suspends federalism whenever it matters, the advantages may hold only in normal times.
> - **The label says little.** Federal states differ greatly. Austria's *Länder* have few powers, the Swiss cantons many. "Federal or unitary" is a scale, and the yes-or-no classification hides most of the variation.
> - **Disadvantaged minorities inside a member state.** Federalism protects a minority that is a majority somewhere. A minority that is scattered, or that is a minority inside its canton, gains nothing and may lose the protection of national rules.
> - Only the third point restates the lecture. The others are my own reading of its table and case.

## Key Connections

- [[Democratic State]]: federalism is one of the marks by which democracies differ, and part of what makes Switzerland a consensus democracy.
- [[Separation of Powers]]: federalism is the vertical direction. The horizontal one divides power among legislature, executive and judiciary.
- [[Sovereign Territorial State]]: in a federal state sovereignty towards the outside is undivided, while state authority inside is shared between levels.
- [[Mission Command]]: subsidiarity and mission command follow the same rule in different settings: decide at the lowest level that has the knowledge.
- [[Political System Model]]: federalism multiplies the central decision system. Each level has its own inputs, outputs and feedback.
- [[Federal Vote of 29 November 2026]]: three of its four proposals need a majority of the cantons as well as of the people, which shows federalism as a veto.
