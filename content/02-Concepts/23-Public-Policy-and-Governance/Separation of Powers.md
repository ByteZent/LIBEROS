> [!bluf]
> Separation of powers spreads state power over several offices and organs, so that **no holder of power can alone take and carry out decisions that change the political system**. The result is a system of **checks and balances** that prevents the concentration of power.
>
> It runs in two directions.
> - **Horizontal:** among legislature, executive and judiciary at one level.
> - **Vertical:** between the national level and the levels below it.
>
> Democracies differ in how far they go in each.
> - Horizontally, presidential systems separate the powers and parliamentary systems fuse them.
> - Vertically, federal states divide power and unitary states concentrate it.

## Definition

**Effective separation of powers:** political power is distributed among different offices and organs of the state, and no holder of power can, independently of the others, take and carry out decisions that change the political system as such [@bernauer2025einfuhrung].

Democracies limit state power by three principles:
- free and fair elections
- the **rule of law**
- the **separation of powers**.

**In my words:** elections decide who holds power, the rule of law binds them to the rules, and the separation of powers makes sure that nobody holds all of it at once.

## Key Points

### Three limits on state power

| Principle | What it does | Why it is not enough alone |
|---|---|---|
| **Free and fair elections** | Those who govern can be removed by the governed | Between two elections a government could still do what it likes |
| **Rule of law** (*Rechtsstaat*) | Every decision-maker is subject to the existing law: nobody stands above it, and a new law must follow the rules of the political process | Law binds, but those who hold all power could change the law |
| **Separation of powers** | Power is spread, so that each holder needs the others | |

The second column is from the textbook. The third is my own reasoning, except the textbook's statement that the rule of law alone does not limit power effectively.

### The two directions

| | Horizontal | Vertical |
|---|---|---|
| **Between whom** | Legislature, executive and judiciary of one level | The national level and the levels below it (cantons, *Länder*) or above it (the EU) |
| **Question** | Who makes, carries out and judges the law? | At which level is a matter decided? |
| **Variants** | Presidential, parliamentary, semi-presidential | Federal, unitary |
| **Strong in** | Presidential systems | Federal states |
| **Weak in** | Parliamentary systems | Unitary states |
| **Examples** | United States · United Kingdom, Germany | Switzerland, Germany, United States · United Kingdom |

### Horizontal separation

The central political decision system has three parts:

| Power | Institutions | Task |
|---|---|---|
| **Legislature** | Parliament | Makes the laws |
| **Executive** | Government and administration | Carries them out |
| **Judiciary** | Courts | Judges by them |

**Systems of government** differ on two features: how the government is elected, and whether it depends on the confidence of parliament.

![[democracy-systems-of-government.svg]]

| System | Election of the executive | Removal | Powers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Parliamentary** | Indirect: only parliament is elected by the people, and the government comes out of parliament | Parliament can bring the government down by a vote of no confidence | Fused: government and parliamentary majority depend on each other | United Kingdom, Germany |
| **Presidential** | The president is elected by the people, separately from parliament | Fixed terms: parliament can remove the president only in extreme cases, and the president cannot dissolve parliament | Clearly separated | United States |
| **Semi-presidential** | A president elected by the people, and a prime minister with a cabinet | The cabinet is responsible to parliament | President and prime minister share executive power | France (strong president), Austria (weak president) |

**Parliamentary and presidential in practice**

| | Parliamentary | Presidential |
|---|---|---|
| **Parties** | Well organised, strong party and parliamentary-group discipline. Parties structure both steps of delegation: voters to members of parliament, and members to the government | Discipline in the legislature tends to be weaker |
| **Agenda-setting** | With the government | Mainly inside the legislature |
| **Ministers** | Strong position | Weaker position |
| **Special rights** | Confidence question (*Vertrauensfrage*) and dissolution of parliament | None: legislature and executive are largely independent of each other |

Why the two systems differ in this way:

- **Party discipline follows from the risk.** In a parliamentary system a member of the governing parties who votes against the government can bring it down and trigger new elections, and so risks the own seat. In a presidential system both sides have fixed terms. Members follow their constituency more, and majorities change from vote to vote.
- **The confidence question** is how a government forces through a disputed bill of its own: it declares that a rejection counts as a vote of no confidence. Its members of parliament must then choose between agreeing and the fall of the government, and mostly agree.
- **The constructive vote of no confidence** (Germany) lets parliament remove the chancellor only by electing a successor at the same time. The country is never without a government. It rather strengthens the government, because the opposition must agree on an alternative and not only on a rejection. The judgement is mine.
- **A minority government** has no majority behind it but is tolerated by one, and can act within limits.
- **Not all parliamentary systems are alike.** The British prime minister leads a one-party government and the party, and so has a strong position in the cabinet. Where coalitions govern, as in Italy, the ministers of the partners have a weight of their own. In Austria the chancellor cannot dissolve parliament, so parliament is stronger towards the government.
- **Cohabitation** (France): president and prime minister belong to different camps. The executive has two heads.

Five cases from the lecture show what each system does:

| System | Case | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Parliamentary | **United Kingdom 2022:** Truss is replaced by Sunak without a new election | The government depends on the parliamentary majority and its party: fusion of powers |
| Parliamentary | **Germany 1982:** constructive vote of no confidence, Schmidt → Kohl | Parliament can change the government during the term |
| Presidential | **United States 2018/19:** government shutdown of 35 days | President and Congress cannot remove each other. With divided government, deadlock threatens |
| Semi-presidential | **France 2024:** Macron dissolves the National Assembly. In December the Barnier government falls to a vote of no confidence | Two centres of power: the president can dissolve parliament, parliament can bring down the government |
| Special case | **Swiss Federal Council:** elected by parliament, cannot be removed for four years | Neither parliamentary nor presidential: a directorial system |

**Effects of the system of government.** There is little theory on why states have the system they have, and more research on what it does.

| | Parliamentary | Presidential |
|---|---|---|
| **Style of politics** | Marked political cooperation | |
| **Stability** | Stable | Higher chance that the system breaks down |
| **Economic and social policy** | Higher taxes and public spending, redistribution, public goods | Lower taxes and spending, less redistribution, fewer public goods. Spending goes more to private goods for particular groups |

- **Why presidential systems might be less stable.** Four arguments from the textbook:
    - **dual legitimacy:** president and parliament are both elected by the people and make competing claims;
    - **deadlock** when different parties control the two;
    - **winner takes all:** one person and one party win the presidency, which can make them intolerant of an opposition that then turns to means outside parliament;
    - **weaker parties:** populist outsiders are elected more easily, and the moderating effect of parties is missing.
- **Why presidential systems might tax and spend less.** The governing party is clearly identifiable, so voters can discipline it more easily than a coalition. And with weaker party discipline, politicians prefer spending for particular clienteles to expensive public goods for all.
- **Are presidential systems really less stable?** Presidential democracies do break down more often. But the system need not be the cause. Presidential systems arise far more often after military regimes (66 % against 28 %), and democracies that follow a military regime last only about 20 years on average instead of about 90, whatever their system. The history explains both. The direct effect of the system is much smaller than the correlation suggests [@cheibub2007presidentialism].
- This is the standard case of a **third variable**: see [[Bias in Research]] on confounding.
- **A second caution:** the differences inside each type are large, and other institutions such as the electoral system and the party system work alongside.

Horizontal separation is therefore **strongest in presidential systems** (*separation of power*) and weakest in parliamentary ones, where legislature and executive are interlocked (*fusion of power*).

**Switzerland: a mixed form.** It fits the typology of systems of government only to a limited extent:

- **Election:** both chambers together (the United Federal Assembly) elect the Federal Council, as in a parliamentary system.
- **No removal:** parliament cannot force the Federal Council to resign during the term, and the Federal Council cannot dissolve parliament. In this the relation resembles a **presidential** system.
- **Collegial government:** seven members with equal rights. There is no separate office of president: one of the seven holds it for a year, with mainly representative duties.
- **Magic formula** (*Zauberformel*): the four largest parties share the seats according to their strength over the last decades, so that elections have no direct effect on the composition of the government.
- **Direct democracy:** very strong rights of the citizens to decide on issues in frequent popular votes.

Outside the textbook this arrangement is usually called a **directorial system**.

**The EU** does not fit the typology either. The Commission is the only organ that may propose laws, which gives it strong influence on legislation. The European Parliament is weaker than a national one: it cannot propose laws, it decides fully only in some fields, and no law passes without the Council. Party discipline is weaker too, because members depend on their national parties and no government depends on their votes. The last reason is my own addition.

### Vertical separation

In a **unitary state** the main decisions are taken at the national level. In a **federal state** the national level and the member states each decide finally in some areas. The typical features, the advantages and disadvantages and the COVID-19 case are in [[Federalism]].

### Where the two cross

- **Every level is separated again.** Because the member states of a federal state decide alone in some fields, they need their own democratically elected executive, legislature and judiciary. Exception: in Austria the judiciary is entirely federal.
- **Each separation adds veto players.** A second chamber, a president elected separately, a constitutional court or the member states are actors whose agreement a reform may need. More veto players, and veto players further apart, mean more stable policy and harder reform (see [[Democratic State#Types of democracy]]).
- **Combinations differ.** The two directions are independent of each other:

| State | Horizontal | Vertical |
|---|---|---|
| United Kingdom | Fused (parliamentary) | Unitary |
| Germany | Fused (parliamentary) | Federal |
| United States | Separated (presidential) | Federal |
| Switzerland | Mixed form | Federal, the strongest of the three German-speaking states |

The table is my own combination of the textbook's examples.

## Origins & Evolution

This section is from general knowledge, not from the course.

1. **Locke** (1690) separates the power to make laws from the power to carry them out.
2. **Montesquieu** (*De l'esprit des lois*, 1748) names three powers and argues that liberty is safe only where power stops power.
3. **United States, 1787:** the constitution applies both directions at once: three branches that check each other, and a federal state. The *Federalist Papers* defend this as a double security.
4. **Switzerland, 1848:** the federal constitution creates the three federal authorities and keeps the cantons as states with their own powers.
5. **Today:** the debate is about **democratic backsliding**: elected governments that weaken courts and parliaments from the inside, without abolishing them.

## Application

- **Analysing a reform.** Ask in both directions who must agree. Horizontally: government, both chambers, possibly a court. Vertically: the member states. In Switzerland a constitutional change needs a majority of the people and of the cantons.
- **Judging a regime.** A state with elections but without effective separation of powers is a democracy only under the narrow definition (see [[Democratic State]]). In an [[Autocratic State]] parliament and courts may exist, but they do not check the ruler.
- **Crisis and emergency.** A crisis moves power in both directions at once. In the "extraordinary situation" of spring 2020 the Swiss Federal Council decided for the whole country: horizontally power moved from parliament to the executive, vertically from the cantons to the Confederation.
- **Civilian control of the armed forces.** The separation of powers applies to the army as well. In Switzerland the army is subordinate to elected authorities, and there is no general in peacetime: the Federal Assembly elects one only when a large part of the army is called up.

The last two points are my own reading.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Fusion in parliamentary systems.** Where the government comes out of the parliamentary majority, the two do not check each other much. The effective check is the opposition, the second chamber and the courts.
> - **Deadlock.** Strong separation can block decisions. In the US government shutdown of 2018/19 president and Congress disagreed for 35 days, and neither could remove or overrule the other.
> - **Unclear responsibility.** The more actors share a decision, the harder it is for voters to say who answers for it. Federalism has the same cost.
> - **Courts are not elected.** A court that stops a majority limits power, and it is itself not democratic.
> - **Paper and practice.** A constitution can separate the powers while one party controls all of them. What counts is whether the separation is **effective**.
> - The first, fourth and fifth points are my own.

## Key Connections

- [[Democratic State]]: separation of powers is one of the three principles by which a democracy limits state power, and part of the broad definition of democracy.
- [[Federalism]]: federalism is the vertical separation of powers. Its features, advantages and disadvantages are there, and this note shows how the two directions cross.
- [[Autocratic State]]: an autocracy concentrates what a democracy separates. Parliaments and courts may exist there without checking the ruler.
- [[Political System Model]]: the three powers together are the central decision system that turns inputs into binding decisions.
- [[Polity, Policy, Politics]]: the separation of powers is part of the *polity*, the institutional frame in which politics takes place.

## Self-Test: Separation of Powers

> [!qard]- 1. What does effective separation of powers mean?
> Power is spread over several offices and organs, and no holder of power can alone take and carry out decisions that change the political system. The result is checks and balances.

> [!qard]- 2. By which three principles do democracies limit state power?
> Free and fair elections, the rule of law, and the separation of powers.

> [!qard]- 3. What is the difference between horizontal and vertical separation of powers?
> Horizontal: among legislature, executive and judiciary of one level. Vertical: between the national level and the levels below it.

> [!qard]- 4. Is horizontal separation stronger in presidential or in parliamentary systems, and why?
> In presidential systems: president and parliament are elected separately and cannot remove each other. In parliamentary systems the government comes out of parliament and depends on its majority (fusion of power).

> [!qard]- 5. Parliamentary or presidential: what is the key difference?
> Two features: how the government is elected, and whether it depends on parliament's confidence.
> - **Parliamentary:** the government comes out of parliament and can be brought down by a vote of no confidence.
> - **Presidential:** the president is elected by the people for a fixed term, cannot be removed by parliament except in extreme cases, and cannot dissolve it.

> [!qard]- 6. What is a semi-presidential system?
> A president elected by the people and a prime minister whose cabinet is responsible to parliament share executive power. Example: France.

> [!qard]- 7. Why does Switzerland fit neither the parliamentary nor the presidential type?
> Parliament elects the Federal Council (parliamentary), but cannot remove it during the term, and the Federal Council cannot dissolve parliament (presidential).

> [!qard]- 8. What marks the rule of law, and why is it not enough alone?
> Every decision-maker is subject to the existing law, and nobody stands above it. It does not by itself prevent the concentration of power: that needs the separation of powers.

> [!qard]- 10. Apply: place the United Kingdom, the United States and Switzerland in both directions.
> <!-- qard-write -->
> United Kingdom: fused (parliamentary) and unitary. United States: separated (presidential) and federal. Switzerland: a mixed form horizontally (elected by parliament, not removable) and strongly federal.

> [!qard]- 11. Why do member states of a federal state have their own three powers?
> Because they decide alone in some policy fields, and so need their own democratically elected executive, legislature and judiciary. Exception: Austria's judiciary is federal.

> [!qard]- 12. Presidential democracies break down more often. Why is the system of government probably not the main cause?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> A third variable: presidential systems follow military regimes far more often (66 % against 28 %), and democracies after military regimes are short-lived under any system (about 20 years against 90). The history explains both (Cheibub).

> [!qard]- 13. Why is party discipline stronger in parliamentary systems?
> A vote against the government can bring it down and trigger new elections, so members of the governing parties risk their own seats. In a presidential system both sides have fixed terms and nothing falls.

> [!qard]- 14. Name four arguments why presidential systems might be less stable.
> Dual legitimacy, deadlock when different parties control president and parliament, winner takes all, and weaker parties that let populist outsiders through.

> [!qard]- 15. What does a government usually want with a confidence question?
> To force through a disputed bill of its own. A rejection would count as a vote of no confidence, so its members of parliament mostly agree.

> [!qard]- 16. Judge: does more separation of powers always make a democracy better?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> No. It limits power, and it costs speed and clarity: more veto players mean harder reform, possible deadlock (US shutdown) and less clear responsibility. How much is right depends on what is feared more, abuse of power or inability to act.
