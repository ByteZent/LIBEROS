> [!bluf]
> An autocratic state is one in which **the rulers cannot be removed by the ruled**. Power is held by one person or a small group, access to power is closed or rigged, and the limits on power (courts, parliament, media) are weak or controlled.
>
> Autocracies are not all alike. They differ in **who rules** (a party, the military, a monarch, one person) and in **how far control reaches** (authoritarian or totalitarian). Many hold elections that the government cannot lose.
>
> To understand how an autocracy behaves, ask what keeps it in power. The usual answer has three parts: **legitimation, repression and co-optation**.

## Definition

Autocracy is usually defined negatively, as the absence of what makes a [[Democratic State]]: no free and fair elections with a real chance of a change of power.

A positive definition asks three questions about rule:

| Question | Democracy | Autocracy |
|---|---|---|
| **Access to power** | Open: free, fair, competitive elections | Closed or rigged |
| **Structure of power** | Divided among several powers that check each other | Concentrated in one person or a small group |
| **Limits of power** | Bound by law and basic rights | Weakly bound or unbound |

In my own words: in an autocracy the government decides whether it stays. In a democracy the voters do.

## Key Points

- **Authoritarian is not the same as totalitarian** [@linz2000totalitarian].

    | | Authoritarian | Totalitarian |
    |---|---|---|
    | Pluralism | Limited, some groups tolerated | None, one centre of power |
    | Ideology | Vague mentalities (order, nation) | A worked-out ideology that claims to explain everything |
    | Mobilisation | Prefers a passive population | Demands active participation |
    | Reach | Controls politics | Tries to control all of life |
    | Examples | Franco's Spain, many military regimes | National Socialist Germany, the Soviet Union under Stalin, North Korea |

- **The three most common kinds** in the course textbook [@bernauer2025einfuhrung]:

    | Kind | Who holds power |
    |---|---|
    | **Monarchy** (*Monarchie*) | A ruling family, succession by descent |
    | **Military regime** (*Militärregime*) | The armed forces |
    | **Civilian regime** (*ziviles Regime*) | Civilians who are neither monarchs nor officers: typically a party or a single leader |

    The finer typology below splits the civilian regimes into single-party and personalist ones. The lecture's chart of the number of autocracies from 1946 to 2010 uses the same four types: military regimes, party dictatorships, monarchies and personalist autocracies.

- **How the four types developed** (read off the lecture's chart, so the numbers are rough). Party dictatorships were the most common type, with a peak of about 50 around 1980 and a fall to about 25 after 1990. Personalist autocracies rose steadily to about 25. Military regimes peaked at about 20 in the 1970s and have almost disappeared. Monarchies stayed at about 10.

- **Who rules matters** [@geddes1999what]:

    | Type | Who holds power | Typical feature |
    |---|---|---|
    | **Single-party** | A party organisation | Most durable: the party settles succession and shares out offices (China) |
    | **Military** | The officer corps, a junta | Often short-lived: officers value the unity of the army over staying in government |
    | **Personalist** | One leader and his circle | Unpredictable, institutions hollowed out, often ends violently |
    | **Monarchy** | A ruling family | Succession by descent |

- **Elections without choice.** Many autocracies hold multi-party elections. In **competitive authoritarian** regimes the opposition is real and sometimes wins seats, but the field is tilted so far (media, courts, state resources) that the government does not lose [@levitsky2010competitive]. These **hybrid regimes** fill the grey zone between democracy and closed autocracy.
- **Three pillars of stability** [@gerschewski2013three]:
    - **Legitimation:** reasons for people to accept the regime (ideology, nationalism, economic performance, security).
    - **Repression:** punishing or deterring opposition, from censorship to violence.
    - **Co-optation:** tying elites and key groups to the regime with offices, money and privileges.
- **The small winning coalition.** Every ruler needs supporters to stay in power. A democratic leader needs a large share of voters and so has to provide public goods. An autocrat needs a small group (generals, party leaders, oligarchs) and can keep it loyal with private rewards [@buenodemesquita2003logic].

### The ruler's arithmetic

[[Selectorate Theory]] turns this into a calculation with two numbers: the **selectorate (S)**, everyone with a say in who rules, and the **winning coalition (W)**, those whose support the ruler needs. Private goods cost more with every member of W, public goods cost the same. In the lecture's example a junta of 100 pays CHF 200,000 in private goods where public goods would cost CHF 1 billion. **What decides is the size of W, not whether the ruler is good or bad.**

The **[[Median Voter Theorem|median voter theorem]]** reaches the same prediction by another route: in an autocracy only the elite counts, and its median position differs from that of the whole population, for example less spending on education. The two explanations are compared in [[Democratic State#Does democracy solve problems better?]].

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Old forms:** monarchy, tyranny and oligarchy are the normal case in history. Democracy is the exception.
2. **20th century:** totalitarianism appears as a new form, made possible by mass parties, mass media and modern police.
3. **After 1945:** research turns to the many regimes that are neither democratic nor totalitarian. Linz gives them the name "authoritarian".
4. **After 1989:** many expected autocracies to disappear. Instead they adapted: elections, parliaments and private media are kept as a front, while control is exercised behind it.
5. **Today:** research focuses on how autocracies stay stable, how they use information technology for control, and how democracies slide into autocracy step by step.

## Application

**Reading an autocracy as an analyst.**

- **Find the pillars.** Which mix of legitimation, repression and co-optation holds the regime up? A regime that relies on economic performance is vulnerable to a recession. One that relies on repression depends on the loyalty of its security forces.
- **Find the winning coalition.** Whose support does the ruler need? Sanctions that hit this group work on the ruler. Sanctions that hit the population may not.
- **Find the regime type.** A party regime, a junta and a personal dictatorship take decisions differently and fail differently.

**Autocracies and their armies.**

- A ruler who fears a coup more than a foreign enemy organises the army against coups: promotion by loyalty, several rival security services, restricted training and communication between units. This **coup-proofing** lowers battlefield performance [@talmadge2015dictators].
- The link to [[Attrition vs Manoeuvre Warfare]]: manoeuvre needs initiative at low levels and honest reporting upwards. Both are exactly what a coup-proofed army suppresses. The same holds for [[OODA Loop|Boyd's loop]]: orientation fails when nobody dares to report bad news.
- **Information control cuts both ways.** Censorship protects the regime from its people, and it also keeps the ruler badly informed.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **A residual category.** "Autocracy" lumps together everything that is not democracy: Saudi Arabia, China, a Sahel junta and North Korea. The differences among them may be larger than the differences between them and some democracies.
> - **The typologies overlap.** Real regimes are mixtures. A leader can turn a party regime into a personal one, and coders disagree about when that has happened.
> - **Totalitarianism as a Cold War concept.** Critics say the term was used to put fascism and communism in one box for political reasons, and that it overstates how total control ever was.
> - **Not always weak in war.** Autocracies that face a serious external threat and little coup risk can build very effective armies. Regime type alone does not predict military performance.
> - **The view from democracies.** Most of the research is written in democracies, with data that autocracies try to hide or falsify. Confidence should be lower than the neat tables suggest.

## Key Connections

- [[Democratic State]]: the other end of the same scale. The grey zone between them (hybrid regimes, defective democracies) is where most classification disputes sit.
- [[Political System Model]]: in an autocracy the input side is controlled from above. Articulation and aggregation are steered or faked, and feedback reaches the rulers only in distorted form.
- [[Sovereign Territorial State]]: an autocracy can be fully sovereign. Sovereignty says nothing about who holds the state authority.
- [[Cognitive Warfare]]: autocracies shield their own information space while using the openness of democracies.
- [[Selectorate Theory]]: explains why a ruler with a small winning coalition pays supporters with private goods and neglects the population.
- [[PMESII]]: regime type, pillars of stability and the winning coalition are the core of the political factor when the opponent is an autocracy.

## Self-Test: Autocracy

> [!qard]- 1. What is the core difference between democracy and autocracy?
> Whether the rulers can be removed by the ruled in free and fair elections.

> [!qard]- 2. Name the three questions about rule that separate the two.
> Access to power (open or closed), structure of power (divided or concentrated), limits of power (bound by law and rights or not).

> [!qard]- 3. How does a totalitarian regime differ from an authoritarian one?
> No pluralism at all, a worked-out ideology, active mobilisation of the population and the claim to control all of life. An authoritarian regime tolerates limited pluralism, has no elaborate ideology and prefers a passive population.

> [!qard]- 4. Name the three most common kinds of autocracy in the textbook, and the finer four-type scheme.
> Textbook: monarchies, military regimes, civilian regimes. Finer scheme: single-party, military, personalist, monarchy.

> [!qard]- 5. What are the three pillars of autocratic stability?
> Legitimation, repression, co-optation.

> [!qard]- 6. What is a competitive authoritarian regime?
> A regime with real multi-party elections in which the field is tilted so heavily in favour of the government that it does not lose.

> [!qard]- 7. Why can coup-proofing make an army worse at fighting?
> Promotion by loyalty, rival services and restricted training and communication protect the ruler from his own officers. They also prevent initiative, cooperation between units and honest reporting, which an army needs in war.

> [!qard]- 8. Apply: sanctions are meant to change an autocrat's behaviour. Whom must they hit, and why?
> The small winning coalition (generals, party leaders, oligarchs) whose support keeps him in power and whom he pays with private goods. Sanctions that hit the population may not reach him.

> [!qard]- 9. Compare: why is a single-party regime usually more durable than a personalist one?
> The party settles succession and shares out offices, so elites have a stake in the system. A personalist regime hollows out institutions and depends on one person, so it is unpredictable and often ends violently.
