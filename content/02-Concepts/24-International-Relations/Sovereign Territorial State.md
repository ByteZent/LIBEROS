> [!bluf]
> The sovereign territorial state is the dominant form of political organisation and the building block of the international system. It has **three elements**: a **people** (*Staatsvolk*), a **territory** (*Staatsgebiet*) and **state authority** (*Staatsgewalt*). It is **sovereign** in two directions: **inside**, no authority stands above it; **outside**, no other state may interfere in its internal affairs.
>
> The form grew out of war-making in Europe, was fixed as a principle in 1648, and was spread over the whole world by colonisation and decolonisation. Since 1945 no working alternative has appeared. New states are rare because **existing states decide who is recognised**.

## Definition

**Three-element doctrine** (*Drei-Elemente-Lehre*), going back to Jellinek [@jellinek1900allgemeine] and still the core of the definition of a state in international law:

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Staatsvolk** | The population or citizens of the state |
| **Staatsgebiet** | A clearly bounded territory on which the state claims sovereignty |
| **Staatsgewalt** | The exercise of sovereign acts by state organs: making rules and enforcing them, if needed by force |

**Sovereignty** has two sides:

- **Internal sovereignty:** there is no higher power of disposal than the state on its territory.
- **External sovereignty:** non-interference in the internal affairs of other states.

In my own words: a state is a group of people on a marked piece of land with an organisation that can make and enforce rules there, and that nobody from outside is allowed to overrule.

## Key Points

- **All three elements are needed.** A people without territory, or a territory without effective authority, is not a state in this sense.
- **State authority can be divided inside the state.** In Switzerland the police is mostly cantonal and the federal level is weak in this field. That does not weaken the element: it is the state as a whole that holds the authority.
- **Sovereignty is a claim that others must accept.** Internal sovereignty is a fact on the ground. External sovereignty depends on **recognition** by other states.
- **The number of states grew in two jumps:** after 1945 (decolonisation) and after 1990 (end of the Soviet Union and Yugoslavia). From a few dozen around 1800 to almost 200 today.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Early political organisation.** Simple forms exist from the 6th millennium BC, mainly in Mesopotamia. The Code of Hammurabi (about 1750 BC) is an early example of written, generally binding rules.
2. **War makes states.** Rulers who wanted to wage war had to extract resources: taxes and soldiers. Extraction needed administration, ways of settling conflicts with the local population, and also their protection. War-making and state-building reinforced each other [@tilly1985war].
3. **Three competitors around 1500.** At the end of the Middle Ages Europe had three forms of organisation: the sovereign territorial state (France, England), city leagues (the Hanse) and city-states (in Italy). The territorial state won [@spruyt1994sovereign].
4. **Peace of Westphalia (1648).** It counts as the birth of the modern state system: rulers stop supporting like-minded religious groups on the territory of other rulers. This is the principle of non-interference.
5. **Export by colonisation.** European powers carried the form to the Americas, Asia, Oceania and Africa. The colonies later became independent as territorial states, inside the colonial borders.
6. **1960: self-determination without conditions.** A resolution of the UN General Assembly on decolonisation separated the right of peoples to self-determination from the ability to organise themselves. Lack of preparedness was no longer a reason to delay independence.

### Expansion of state functions

- **Night-watchman state** (*Nachtwächterstaat*, a term coined by Lassalle in 1862 as a criticism): the state does three things only. It keeps order inside, protects people and territory against the outside, and sets a frame for the economy (property rights, financial system).
- **After the Great Depression** of the 1920s and 1930s the state took on economic and social steering: the **welfare state** and **industrial policy**.
- **Measure:** the *Staatsquote*, the share of government spending (or taxes) in GDP. In most rich industrial states it lies between 30 and 60 per cent. Social security and education take the largest part.
- **Why it also grew in capitalist states:** in East and West, effective economic and social steering was seen as a condition for military readiness. Agricultural policy and energy policy are examples.
- **Since 1990:** states handed competences upwards (supranational organisations) and downwards (markets, NGOs). Whether geopolitical rivalry now brings them back is open.

## Application

**Why have no working alternatives appeared since 1945?**
- Colonisation had already made the territorial state the only available model.
- Many newer states have external recognition but lack internal sovereignty. They are **quasi-states** [@jackson1990quasistates]: weak copies of the model, not alternatives to it.

**Why are there not hundreds of new states, despite the 1960 resolution?**
- Existing states act as **gatekeepers**. A new state needs recognition, and UN membership needs a recommendation of the Security Council, where five states hold a veto, and a vote in the General Assembly.
- Examples of entities that are blocked or only partly recognised: Palestine, Northern Cyprus, Abkhazia, South Ossetia, Kosovo.
- Recognition is a large advantage for a new state. This pushes every candidate to look like the standard model, so states become more and more alike.

**Security reading.** The three elements are also three targets. A state can be attacked through its **territory** (occupation, annexation), its **people** (influence on what the population believes and whom it is loyal to, see [[Cognitive Warfare]]) or its **authority** (making it unable to enforce its rules). Defence in the wide sense protects all three.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **The "Westphalian" story is partly a myth.** The treaties of 1648 say little about sovereignty in the modern sense. The link was made much later [@osiander2001sovereignty].
> - **Organised hypocrisy.** Rulers have always violated the sovereignty of others when it suited them, and the norm survived anyway. Sovereignty is better seen as several things (legal recognition, non-interference, control inside, control of borders) that often do not come together [@krasner1999sovereignty].
> - **Three elements describe, they do not decide.** Whether recognition *creates* a state or only *confirms* one is disputed in international law. Kosovo and Taiwan show that the elements alone do not settle the matter.
> - **The EU fits badly.** Member states have handed over parts of their authority without ceasing to be states.
> - **A European template.** Applying it worldwide treats other historical forms of rule (empires, tributary systems, nomadic orders) as deviations.

## Self-Test: Territorial State

> [!qard]- 1. Explain the three-element doctrine.
> A state consists of a people (population or citizens), a territory (a clearly bounded area on which it claims sovereignty) and state authority (sovereign acts exercised by state organs).

> [!qard]- 2. What is the difference between internal and external sovereignty?
> Internal: no power on the territory stands above the state. External: other states do not interfere in its internal affairs.

> [!qard]- 3. In what sense did war make the state?
> War needed taxes and soldiers. To extract them, rulers had to build administration, settle conflicts with the population and protect it. These organisations became the state, which in turn could wage more war.

> [!qard]- 4. Have working alternatives to the sovereign territorial state appeared since 1945? Why (not)?
> No. Colonisation exported the form worldwide. Self-determination was separated from the ability to self-organise, which produced many quasi-states. They lack internal sovereignty, so they are incomplete versions of the model, not alternatives.

> [!qard]- 5. Why have hundreds of new states not appeared since 1960?
> Existing states act as gatekeepers: they can refuse recognition, in the UN Security Council and the General Assembly. Examples: Palestine, Northern Cyprus, Abkhazia, South Ossetia, Kosovo.

> [!qard]- 6. How can the size of state activity be measured and compared?
> With the *Staatsquote*: government spending (or taxes) as a share of GDP. Rich industrial states lie between 30 and 60 per cent, most of it for the welfare state.

> [!qard]- 7. Why did the welfare state also grow in capitalist societies?
> Effective economic and social steering was seen in East and West as a precondition for military readiness.

> [!qard]- 8. Apply: an entity controls its territory and population but is recognised by few states. Which side of sovereignty does it have, and what is the reverse case called?
> Internal sovereignty without full external sovereignty, as with Kosovo or Northern Cyprus. The reverse, recognition from outside without effective control inside, is a quasi-state.

> [!qard]- 9. Apply: read the three elements of the state as three targets.
> Territory: occupation or annexation. People: influence on what the population believes and whom it is loyal to. Authority: making the state unable to enforce its rules. Defence in the wide sense protects all three.
