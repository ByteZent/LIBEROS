> [!bluf]
> Memory is described as **three stores**: a **sensory store** that holds impressions for under two seconds, a **short-term (working) memory** that holds a little for a short time, and a **long-term memory** that is permanent but not perfect. Information moves on only through attention and rehearsal.
>
> Three findings matter in practice. We **forget fast at first** and then slowly (the forgetting curve). We remember the **beginning and the end** of a series best. And remembering is **reconstruction**: a memory can be changed afterwards by the way a question is asked. A confident witness is not therefore a correct one.

## Definition

### The three-store model

| Store | Holds | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensory store** | Raw visual, auditory, tactile impressions | Under 2 seconds |
| **Short-term memory** (temporary working memory) | What is currently attended to | About 5 seconds to 20 minutes |
| **Long-term memory** | Permanent store | Lasting, but not perfect |

- A stimulus enters the sensory store. What is **attended to** passes into short-term memory.
- **Rehearsal** keeps it there and transfers it into long-term memory.
- **Retrieval** brings it back from long-term into working memory.
- At every stage information can be **forgotten**.

### Kinds of long-term memory

| Kind | Content | Type | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Episodic** | Events, personal experience | Declarative | When did I first drive a car? |
| **Semantic** | Facts, general knowledge | Declarative | What is a car? |
| **Procedural** | Skills | Non-declarative | How does one drive a car? |

## Key Points

- **Forgetting curve.** Ebbinghaus learned rows of nonsense syllables (to rule out prior knowledge) and measured retention by the **saving** when learning them again. Most is lost soon after learning. After that the curve flattens [@ebbinghaus1885gedachtnis].
- **Learning curve.** The number of repetitions needed rises steeply with the length of the list. Twice as much material needs far more than twice the effort.
- **Primacy and recency effect.** In free recall of a list the first items (already rehearsed into long-term memory) and the last items (still in short-term memory) are remembered best. The middle is lost [@murdock1962serial].
- **Mnemonic techniques:**
    - **chunking**: grouping items into larger units,
    - forming a **mental image**,
    - the **method of loci**: placing items along a mental walk,
    - **acrostics**: a sentence whose first letters give the items,
    - **acronyms**.
    - Structure and hierarchy also help.
- **Memory errors.** Credibility does not imply truth. Recollection can be distorted by
    - **suggestive influence** at retrieval,
    - **false information** given afterwards,
    - **low attention** at the time of the event, so that the gaps are filled by reconstruction,
    - **imagination**.
- **Loftus' experiments.**
    - Students watched a film of a car accident. Asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" each other, they estimated a higher speed than those asked about cars that "hit" each other (about 41 versus 34 miles per hour). A week later, more of the first group "remembered" broken glass that was never there [@loftus1974reconstruction].
    - In another study a question mentioned a barn that was not in the film. A week later 17 per cent of that group remembered a barn, against 3 per cent of the others.

## Application

- **Teaching and briefing.**
    - Put the most important point **first and last**. The middle of a long order is what gets lost.
    - **Repeat early.** Because forgetting is steepest right after learning, a short review the same day and again a few days later does more than one long session.
    - **Structure** the material. A fixed order scheme works like a method of loci: every item has its place.
    - Do not overload. Effort grows faster than content.
- **Military mnemonics.** Acronyms and fixed sequences for reports and orders are chunking and acrostics applied. They hold up when working memory is taken up by stress.
- **Skills versus facts.** Weapon handling is procedural memory: learned by doing, hard to put into words, and very durable (see [[Attention and Automatisation]]).
- **Debriefing and investigation.**
    - Take statements **early** and **separately**, before people talk to each other.
    - Ask **open** questions ("what did you see?"), not leading ones ("how fast was the vehicle when it rammed the gate?").
    - Treat confident detail with the same caution as hesitant detail.
    - This applies to after-action reviews, to disciplinary inquiries and to intelligence debriefs.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **The three-store model is dated.** Later research replaced the single short-term store with a working memory made of several components, and showed that depth of processing matters more than mere rehearsal.
> - **Nonsense syllables are not real learning.** Meaningful, connected material is forgotten far more slowly than Ebbinghaus' curve suggests. The curve is a worst case.
> - **Misinformation effects are real but bounded.** They are strongest for peripheral details and weak memories. Central events are remembered better.
> - **Durations in the table are rough.** Without rehearsal, short-term memory fades within seconds, not minutes.

## Key Connections

- [[Attention and Automatisation]]: only what is attended to gets stored. Procedural memory is the result of automatisation.
- [[Perception]]: stored knowledge shapes what is perceived, so errors can enter before anything is stored.
- [[Heuristics and Anchoring]]: a leading question works like an anchor.
- [[Bias in Research]]: recall errors are a source of measurement bias in surveys and interviews.
- [[CREW]]: eyewitness accounts are evidence whose reliability has to be argued, not assumed.

## Self-Test: Memory

> [!qard]- 1. Name the three stores and their durations.
> Sensory store (under 2 seconds), short-term or working memory (seconds to minutes), long-term memory (lasting, but not perfect).

> [!qard]- 2. Name the three kinds of long-term memory, each with an example.
> Episodic (when I first drove a car), semantic (what a car is), procedural (how to drive).

> [!qard]- 3. What does the forgetting curve show, and how was it measured?
> Forgetting is fastest right after learning and then levels off. Measured by the saving in effort when relearning lists of nonsense syllables.

> [!qard]- 4. What is the primacy-recency effect?
> The first and last items of a series are remembered best, the middle worst.

> [!qard]- 5. Name four mnemonic techniques.
> Chunking, mental imagery, the method of loci, acrostics or acronyms.

> [!qard]- 6. What did Loftus show?
> The wording of a question changes the answer and can change the memory itself: a stronger verb raised speed estimates and later produced false memories of broken glass.

> [!qard]- 7. What follows for questioning a witness?
> Ask early, separately and with open questions. Avoid wording that suggests an answer.

> [!qard]- 8. Apply: you give an order with ten points. Where do you put the most important one, and why?
> First and last. In recall, the beginning (already rehearsed into long-term memory) and the end (still in short-term memory) are kept best. The middle gets lost.

> [!qard]- 9. Apply: four hours of theory have to be known for a test in two weeks. One block, or spread out? Why?
> Spread out: a short review the same day and again a few days later. Forgetting is steepest right after learning, and effort grows faster than the amount of material.

> [!qard]- 10. Compare: semantic and procedural memory. What follows for weapon training?
> Semantic memory holds facts that can be stated. Procedural memory holds skills: learned by doing, hard to put into words, very durable. So weapon handling is trained by repetition, not by explanation.
