> [!bluf]
> People want their thoughts, beliefs and actions to **fit together**. When two of them contradict each other ("smoking is unhealthy" and "I smoke"), the result is an unpleasant tension: **cognitive dissonance**. The tension pushes the person to reduce it.
>
> There are three ways: **add** thoughts that fit, **change** the thought that does not fit, or **change the behaviour**. Changing behaviour is the hardest, so people usually change their thinking instead. The practical lesson: **people do not only act on their beliefs, they also adjust their beliefs to what they have done.**

## Question

Why do people hold on to a decision or a habit when the evidence speaks against it, and even grow more convinced?

## Assumptions

- People strive for **balance** in their cognitive system.
- **Cognitions** are any pieces of knowledge, opinion or belief about oneself, one's behaviour or the world.
- Relations between cognitions are **consonant** (fitting) or **dissonant** (contradictory).
- Dissonance is felt as unpleasant and motivates its own reduction.

> [!tip] Test
> The theory needs the person to feel responsible. If the action was forced or trivial, there is little dissonance and little change of attitude. This is why free choice and small rewards produce more attitude change than orders and large rewards.

## Mechanism

| | Cognition 1 | Cognition 2 | Relation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Example A | Sport is murder | I do no sport | Consonant |
| Example B | Smoking is unhealthy | I smoke | Dissonant |

### Three ways to reduce dissonance

| Way | Example for the smoker |
|---|---|
| **1. Add consonant cognitions** | "Smoking relaxes me, so I work better." "Smoking is a pleasure." |
| **2. Change the dissonant cognition** | "Smoking is not that unhealthy. Those studies are overrated." |
| **3. Change the behaviour** | "I no longer smoke." |

Ways 1 and 2 cost nothing but honesty. Way 3 costs effort. So the first two are the usual ones.

## Predictions & Evidence

| Prediction | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| After a hard choice, people rate the chosen option higher and the rejected one lower | Found repeatedly in experiments | supported |
| People paid little to say something they do not believe shift their opinion more than people paid much | Classic experiment by Festinger and Carlsmith | supported |
| The more someone has invested in a group or goal, the more they value it | Found for harsh initiations and for effort in general | supported, with debate about the size |

## Policy Implications

- **Commitment and sunk costs.** A commander who has committed forces to a plan will read new information in its favour. The more was invested, the stronger the pull. Build in a fixed point at which the plan is reviewed by someone who did not make it.
- **Initiation and hardship.** What cost effort is valued more. Hard selection and demanding training raise attachment to the unit. The same mechanism makes people defend degrading rituals they went through themselves.
- **Changing attitudes through behaviour.** If people can be brought to act in a certain way **voluntarily** and with **little outside pressure**, their attitude tends to follow. Heavy pressure produces compliance without conviction.
- **Moral injury.** A soldier who acts against his own values has dissonance of the gravest kind. It can be reduced by devaluing the victim ("they deserved it"), which is how abuses spread, or it stays as lasting distress.
- **Influence operations.** Small first steps (a signature, a shared post) create a commitment that people then justify to themselves. Information that contradicts a strongly held belief is often rejected, and can even strengthen the belief (see [[Cognitive Warfare]]).
- **Leadership.** When a subordinate defends an obviously poor decision, more facts rarely help. Offer a way to change course without losing face.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Alternative explanation.** Self-perception theory says people simply infer their attitudes from watching their own behaviour, with no unpleasant tension needed. The two are hard to tell apart in experiments.
> - **Hard to falsify in its loose form.** Almost any change or non-change of attitude can be described afterwards as dissonance reduction. The theory has to say in advance which way people will take (see [[Falsificationism]]).
> - **Conditions matter.** Effects depend on free choice, foreseeable consequences and threat to the self-image. Without these they are weak.
> - **Cultural range.** The classic effects are weaker where the consistency of the individual self is valued less.
> - **Replication.** Some well-known dissonance findings have turned out smaller in large replications.

## Origins & Evolution

- Festinger formulated the theory in the 1950s [@festinger1957theory].
- It became one of the most influential theories of social psychology and belongs to the cognitive school: behaviour depends on how the person processes the situation (see [[Menschenbilder in Psychology]]).

## Self-Test: Cognitive Dissonance

> [!qard]- 1. What is cognitive dissonance?
> The unpleasant tension that arises when two cognitions contradict each other, which pushes the person to reduce it.

> [!qard]- 2. Name the three ways of reducing dissonance, with the smoker as example.
> Add consonant cognitions ("it relaxes me"), change the dissonant cognition ("it is not that unhealthy"), change the behaviour (stop smoking).

> [!qard]- 3. Which way is chosen least often, and why?
> Changing the behaviour, because it costs the most effort.

> [!qard]- 4. Give one consonant and one dissonant pair of cognitions.
> Consonant: "sport is murder" and "I do no sport". Dissonant: "smoking is unhealthy" and "I smoke".

> [!qard]- 5. Apply it: why does a commander hold on to a failing plan?
> Admitting failure contradicts "I am a competent leader" and the investment already made. It is easier to reinterpret the information than to change the plan.

> [!qard]- 6. Apply: you want soldiers to accept a new safety rule, not just obey it. Heavy sanctions, or a voluntary commitment with little pressure? Why?
> Voluntary action with little outside pressure. The person has to justify the behaviour to himself, so the attitude follows it. Heavy pressure produces compliance without conviction.

> [!qard]- 7. Compare: dissonance theory and self-perception theory.
> Dissonance theory: an unpleasant tension drives the change of attitude. Self-perception theory: people simply infer their attitude from watching their own behaviour, with no tension needed. Experiments struggle to tell them apart.
