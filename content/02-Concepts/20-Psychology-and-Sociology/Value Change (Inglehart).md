> [!bluf]
> Value change is a shift in the **ranking** of values in a society, not the appearance of new values. The best-known theory is Inglehart's: as societies grow rich and secure, priorities move from **materialist** values (order, prosperity, security) to **post-materialist** ones (self-fulfilment, participation, autonomy).
>
> It rests on two hypotheses. **Scarcity:** people value most what is in short supply. **Socialisation:** basic values are formed in youth and then stay. So change comes slowly, through the replacement of generations.
>
> For the armed forces the trend is uncomfortable: the values that lose weight (discipline, obedience, duty) are the ones military organisation is built on.

## Question

Why did the values of Western societies shift after 1945, and why did the shift arrive generation by generation instead of all at once?

## Assumptions

- **Scarcity hypothesis:** a person's priorities reflect their situation. What is scarce is valued most. Once material needs are secure, non-material goals move up.
- **Socialisation hypothesis:** basic values are shaped in the formative years and remain fairly stable afterwards.

> [!tip] Test
> Take away security. If prosperity and safety become uncertain again (war in Europe, inflation, housing costs), the scarcity hypothesis predicts a turn back to materialist values, at least for those growing up now.

## Mechanism

1. Value conflicts raise the social pressure to change the ranking of values.
2. A generation that grows up in prosperity and peace takes material security for granted.
3. It gives priority to autonomy, participation and self-expression.
4. Because values formed in youth persist, the society's overall ranking shifts only as older cohorts are replaced by younger ones.

### Value change in Europe since 1945

| Period | Dominant values |
|---|---|
| 1945 to 1960s | **Traditional:** law and order, performance, sense of duty, discipline |
| 1960s and partly 1970s | **Material:** prosperity, social security, growth |
| 1970s and 1980s | **Alternative:** self-fulfilment, independence, social movements |
| From the 1990s | **Individual:** individualism, hedonism, enjoyment and fun |

### Trends

| Gaining weight: values of individual autonomy | Losing weight: values of external direction |
|---|---|
| **Idealistic:** emancipation, equal treatment, democracy, participation | Discipline, obedience |
| **Hedonistic:** enjoyment, adventure, excitement, variety | Order |
| **Individualistic:** creativity, spontaneity, independence | Loyalty, modesty |

### Drivers

- expansion of education,
- technological and economic progress,
- urbanisation,
- new family models,
- media, internet, social media,
- changed perception of risk,
- social movements (peace, ecology, equality),
- intercultural influence through migration.

## Predictions & Evidence

| Prediction | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Younger cohorts are more post-materialist than older ones | Found in repeated surveys in many Western countries | supported |
| Economic crises push answers back towards materialism | Short-term swings with inflation and unemployment are visible | supported |
| Societies converge on post-materialism as they grow rich | Mixed: culture and religion leave lasting differences | mixed |

## Policy Implications

- **Recruitment and motivation.** A force cannot count on duty and obedience being accepted as values in themselves. It has to offer meaning, participation and personal development.
- **Leadership.** Orders need reasons more often. Authority comes less from rank and more from competence and example.
- **Institution or occupation.** As civilian values spread, military service is seen less as a calling with its own values and more as a job compared with other jobs [@moskos1977institution].
- **Not a one-way street.** If security becomes scarce again, readiness to serve and acceptance of defence spending can rise quickly.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **One dimension is too few.** People can hold materialist and post-materialist values at once. Other researchers find mixed types and several dimensions.
> - **Measured by a ranking question.** The classic survey item forces a choice among a few goals. The result depends heavily on which goals are offered.
> - **Period effects versus generation effects.** If everyone's answers move with the economy, the claim that values are fixed in youth is weakened.
> - **Western bias.** The theory generalises from the post-war West.
> - **The period table is a simplification.** Decades do not have one set of values. Groups in the same decade differ more than decades do.

## Self-Test: Value Change

> [!qard]- 1. What changes in a value change?
> The ranking of values, not the values themselves.

> [!qard]- 2. State Inglehart's two hypotheses.
> Scarcity: what is scarce is valued most. Socialisation: basic values are formed in youth and remain stable.

> [!qard]- 3. Why does value change proceed slowly?
> Because values formed in youth persist, the overall ranking shifts mainly through the replacement of generations.

> [!qard]- 4. Which values gain and which lose weight?
> Gaining: values of autonomy (idealistic, hedonistic, individualistic). Losing: values of external direction such as discipline, obedience, order, loyalty, modesty.

> [!qard]- 5. Name four drivers of value change.
> Expansion of education, technological and economic progress, urbanisation, media and social media (also: family models, risk perception, social movements, migration).

> [!qard]- 6. What does the theory predict when security becomes scarce again?
> A move back towards materialist values, most lasting among those in their formative years.

> [!qard]- 7. Apply: duty and obedience lose weight as values. What follows for recruitment and leadership?
> A force cannot count on them being accepted as values in themselves. It has to offer meaning, participation and personal development, and authority has to rest on competence and example more than on rank.

> [!qard]- 8. Judge: in surveys, the answers of all age groups swing with the economy. Why does that weaken the theory?
> That is a period effect. If values move with the present for every age group, the socialisation hypothesis, that basic values are fixed in youth, is weakened.
