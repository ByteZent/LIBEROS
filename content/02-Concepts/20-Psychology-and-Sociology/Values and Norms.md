> [!bluf]
> **Values** are general, collectively shared ideas of what is **desirable**. **Norms** are the concrete, binding **expectations** that society places on the individual. Values say what is good. Norms say what to do.
>
> Together they make **social order** possible: they tell me how to act and let me predict how others will act. In an open society norms are never fixed once and for all. They are under constant pressure to justify themselves, and that is where **value change** starts.

## Definition

| | Values (*Werte*) | Norms (*Normen*) |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | General ideas of the desirable | Instructions for action, expectations of society towards the individual |
| **Binding?** | Guide action as "signposts" | Binding, backed by sanctions |
| **Level** | Abstract | Concrete |
| **Example** | Safety, freedom, loyalty | Speed limits, saluting, queuing |

**Values**, more fully:
- are **collectively shared**,
- **guide action**,
- are organised in **value systems** with higher and lower ranks (leading values),
- contribute to social **cohesion** and to the **legitimation** of the social order,
- are **plural** in modern societies, which is a source of value conflicts.

**"Social" in sociology** is value-neutral: it means any behaviour between people. It does not mean "charitable" (the moral sense) or "socialist" (the political sense).

## Key Points

- **Norms take many forms:** laws, prohibitions, customs (*Sitten*), habits (*Gebräuche*). They range from written and enforced by the state to unwritten and enforced by a look.
- **Relief function** (*Entlastungsfunktion*). Norms give orientation in daily life. I do not have to weigh every action anew, and I can estimate what others will do. "Being the only one who does it differently just feels awkward."
- **What is "normal" is relative.** An action is normal for a given group in a given period if it is seen as normal on average across the society of that time. The same act can be a baptism, a trade ritual or a witch trial, depending on context.
- **From explicit to implicit.** Opinions and attitudes sit near the surface and can be stated. Motives, values, norms and needs lie deeper and are often not conscious.
- **Constant pressure to legitimate.** In an open society any norm can be questioned. When value conflicts build up, the ranking of values shifts (see [[Value Change (Inglehart)]]).

## Origins & Evolution

- Sociology arose with the Enlightenment and the French Revolution, when the old order stopped being taken for granted and "how can people live together at all?" became a real question. Comte coined the word.
- The classic answer is that shared values and norms, passed on through **socialisation**, hold a society together.

## Application

- **The armed forces run on norms.** Regulations, orders, drill and ritual are norms in the strict sense: explicit, binding, sanctioned. Next to them stand strong unwritten norms of the unit (comradeship, not leaving anyone behind).
- **Relief function under stress.** Standard procedures do for a unit what norms do for a society: nobody has to think about the basics, and everyone can predict what the others will do.
- **Where friction comes from.** Recruits bring the values of civil society with them. Where these differ from military norms (individual autonomy versus obedience), the leader has to explain the norm, not only enforce it. A norm that cannot be justified loses its hold.
- **Rituals** such as initiation rites are norms of the group. They can integrate, and they can turn into bullying. The same act reads differently from inside and outside.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Consensus bias.** The picture of shared values holding society together underplays power. Conflict theories ask whose values became the norms, and who gains from them.
> - **Values do not predict behaviour well.** People who state a value often act against it. Situation and group pressure matter (see [[Attribution]] and [[Cognitive Dissonance]]).
> - **Hard to measure.** Values are asked for in surveys, where people give socially desirable answers (see [[Bias in Research]]).
> - **Circular definition of "normal".** Defining normal as what counts as normal on average describes the fact but does not explain how a norm arises or changes.

## Self-Test: Values and Norms

> [!qard]- 1. What is the difference between a value and a norm?
> A value is a general, shared idea of what is desirable. A norm is a concrete, binding expectation of how to act.

> [!qard]- 2. Name five characteristics of values.
> Collectively shared, action-guiding, organised in ranked value systems, contributing to cohesion and legitimation of the social order, plural in modern societies.

> [!qard]- 3. What is the relief function of norms?
> They give orientation, so that one's own action need not be weighed anew each time and the actions of others can be predicted.

> [!qard]- 4. What does "social" mean in sociology?
> Any behaviour between people, in a value-neutral sense.

> [!qard]- 5. Why are norms never fixed in an open society?
> They are under constant pressure to justify themselves and can be questioned at any time.

> [!qard]- 6. Apply: classify loyalty, the salute, and "nobody is left behind".
> Loyalty is a value: an abstract idea of the desirable. The salute is a written, sanctioned norm. "Nobody is left behind" is an unwritten norm of the unit, enforced by the group.

> [!qard]- 7. Apply: why do standard procedures help a unit under stress? Use the relief function.
> Nobody has to weigh the basics anew, and everyone can predict what the others will do. Procedures do for a unit what norms do for a society.

> [!qard]- 8. Limit: in a survey a soldier says comradeship matters most to him. Why does that predict his behaviour only weakly?
> Stated values and behaviour diverge. The situation and group pressure matter, and survey answers lean towards what is socially desirable.
