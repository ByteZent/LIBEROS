> [!bluf]
> The rule of law means that public power is exercised **through and under law**: laws are general, public, prospective, clear and stable; they apply to rulers as well as the ruled; and independent courts enforce them. Everyone agrees on this *thin* core. Whether the concept also includes democracy and fundamental rights (*thick* conceptions) is contested. For policy-making it works as a hard constraint: every instrument needs a **legal basis** and must be **proportionate**.

## Definition

**Venice Commission Rule of Law Checklist (2016)** names five benchmarks: *legality; legal certainty; prevention of abuse of powers; equality before the law and non-discrimination; access to justice*.

**In my words:** the state may only do what the law allows. The law must be knowable in advance, and someone independent must be able to hold the state to it.

## Key Points

- **Dicey (1885):** supremacy of regular law over arbitrary power; equality before the ordinary courts; rights protected through the courts.
- **Fuller (1964): eight formal requirements.** Laws must be general, promulgated, non-retroactive, clear, non-contradictory, possible to comply with, reasonably constant, and actually applied as written. A system that fails them totally is not a legal system at all.
- **Thin vs thick** (Raz vs Bingham): Raz holds that the rule of law is a *formal* virtue compatible with unjust laws. Bingham includes protection of fundamental rights and compliance with international law.
- ***Rechtsstaat*** (the German and Swiss tradition) emphasises **legality** and **proportionality** as the principles that bind all state action.

## Origins & Evolution

From Aristotle's "rule of laws, not of men", through the English common-law tradition (Magna Carta, Dicey) and the continental *Rechtsstaat*, to today's international benchmarks: EU Art. 2 TEU values and rule-of-law conditionality, the Venice Commission, and measurement indices such as the World Justice Project Rule of Law Index and the World Bank WGI.

## Application

**In constitutional practice (example: Switzerland).** Art. 5 of the Federal Constitution makes law "the basis and the limit of state activity" and requires public interest, proportionality and good faith. Art. 36 lets fundamental rights be restricted only with a **legal basis**, a **public interest**, **proportionality**, and without touching their **core content**.

**Proportionality test**, used across European courts:
1. **Suitability:** does the measure serve the aim?
2. **Necessity:** is there a less intrusive alternative?
3. **Proportionality in the narrow sense:** do the benefits outweigh the burden?

**Policy relevance:** it applies at every stage of the [[Policy Cycle]]. Formulation needs a legal basis, implementation has to respect proportionality, and courts provide a form of evaluation.

**Security relevance:** emergency powers, surveillance, domestic military deployment and sanctions are exactly the areas where the tension between security and rule-of-law constraints is sharpest.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Rule *by* law:** authoritarian regimes can meet formal requirements while using law as an instrument of control. Is the thin concept too thin?
> - **Thick concepts overload the term:** folding democracy and rights into "rule of law" makes it less analytically useful (Raz).
> - **Measurement:** indices rely heavily on expert perceptions and embed particular (often Western, common-law) assumptions.
