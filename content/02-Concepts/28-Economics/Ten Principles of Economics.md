> [!bluf]
> Economics studies how a society manages **scarce** resources. Mankiw sums up its basic findings in ten principles in three groups: **how people decide** (1–4), **how people interact** (5–7) and **how the economy as a whole works** (8–10). Principles 1–7 belong to microeconomics, 8–10 to macroeconomics.
>
> The four ideas to carry everywhere: every choice has an **opportunity cost**, decisions are made **at the margin**, people respond to **incentives**, and markets usually allocate well but can **fail**.

## Definition

**Economics** comes from the Greek word for household management. Because goods are scarce, every society has to answer three questions: **what** is produced, **how**, and **for whom**.

| Field | Looks at |
|---|---|
| **Microeconomics** | Decisions of households and firms and their meeting in markets. Classically a theory of prices and markets, today also of pollution, competition, incentives and information |
| **Macroeconomics** | The economy of a country as a whole: growth, inflation, unemployment |
| **Business administration** | The view of the single firm, not of society |

## Key Points

### How people decide

| # | Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **People face trade-offs** | To get one thing we give up another: guns or butter, leisure or income, **efficiency or equality**. "There is no such thing as a free lunch." |
| 2 | **The cost of something is what you give up to get it** | This is the **opportunity cost** (*Opportunitätskosten*): the best alternative forgone |
| 3 | **Rational people think at the margin** | Decisions are small adjustments to an existing plan. Compare **marginal benefit** with **marginal cost** |
| 4 | **People respond to incentives** | When marginal costs or benefits change, behaviour changes |

- **Efficiency:** society gets the most out of its scarce resources (the size of the cake).
- **Equality:** the benefits are distributed uniformly among the members of society (how the cake is cut).

### How people interact

| # | Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | **Trade can make everyone better off** | Trade allows specialisation in what each does best. See [[Comparative Advantage]] |
| 6 | **Markets are usually a good way to organise economic activity** | Many decentralised decisions of households and firms, coordinated by prices, as if led by an **invisible hand** [@smith1776wealth] |
| 7 | **Governments can sometimes improve market outcomes** | When a market does not allocate resources well, there is **market failure** |

- **Causes of market failure:**
    - **Externality:** an action affects the welfare of an uninvolved third party.
    - **Market power:** one person or firm can influence the market price noticeably.
- **Caution:** whether intervention helps also depends on its costs and on **government failure**.

### How the economy as a whole works

| # | Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | **A country's standard of living depends on its ability to produce** | Almost all differences in living standards come from differences in **productivity**: the value of goods and services produced per hour of work |
| 9 | **Prices rise when the government prints too much money** (lecture wording: when the central bank puts too much money into circulation) | **Inflation** is a rise in the general price level, caused by fast growth of the money supply |
| 10 | **Society faces a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment** | More money raises demand, output and employment for a while, and prices with them. The trade-off holds only in the short run |

## Application

- **Opportunity cost in defence.** Every franc for the army is a franc not spent on something else, and every soldier in service is a worker missing in a firm. The [[Production Possibilities Frontier]] draws this as "guns versus butter".
- **Marginal thinking in planning.** The question is rarely "air defence, yes or no?" but "what does one *more* battery add, and what does it cost?"
- **Incentives in leadership.** People adjust to what is rewarded and punished, including in ways nobody intended. A rule that punishes reported mistakes produces fewer reports, not fewer mistakes.
- **Reading the news.** Most economic policy debates turn on principle 7: is there really a market failure, and will the intervention cost less than it gains?

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **A teaching device, not a theory.** The ten principles are one textbook's summary. Other textbooks choose differently.
> - **Rational actors.** Principles 3 and 4 assume people weigh costs and benefits. Psychology shows systematic deviations (see [[Heuristics and Anchoring]]).
> - **"Usually" and "sometimes" carry the weight.** Principles 6 and 7 do not say when markets work and when governments help. That is exactly where economists disagree.
> - **Principles 9 and 10 are contested** in their simple form. Inflation has other causes besides money growth, and the short-run trade-off is unstable.

## Self-Test: Ten Principles

> [!qard]- 1. Name the three groups and which principles belong to each.
> - How people decide (1–4)
> - How people interact (5–7)
> - How the economy as a whole works (8–10)

> [!qard]- 2. What is the opportunity cost of attending a lecture?
> The best alternative use of that time: sleep, paid work or study for another subject. Not the tuition fee, which is paid either way.

> [!qard]- 3. What does "thinking at the margin" mean?
> Comparing the additional benefit and the additional cost of a small change to an existing plan, instead of deciding all or nothing.

> [!qard]- 4. Name two causes of market failure.
> Externalities and market power.

> [!qard]- 5. What explains most differences in living standards between countries?
> Productivity: the value of goods and services produced per hour of work.

> [!qard]- 6. Why is the trade-off between efficiency and equality a trade-off?
> Redistribution changes incentives to work and invest, which can shrink the total. A larger cake and a more equal cut cannot both be maximised.

> [!qard]- 7. Apply: a unit punishes every mistake that is reported. What does principle 4 predict?
> Fewer reports, not fewer mistakes. People respond to incentives, including in ways nobody intended.

> [!qard]- 8. Apply: how does thinking at the margin frame a decision on air defence?
> Not "air defence, yes or no?" but "what does one *more* battery add, and what does it cost?" Compare the marginal benefit with the marginal cost.

> [!qard]- 9. Judge: a market failure has been shown. Does it follow that the government should intervene?
> No. Whether intervention helps depends on what it costs and on government failure. Principle 7 says governments can *sometimes* improve the outcome.
