> [!bluf]
> **Mission command** (*Auftragstaktik*) is a way of commanding in which the superior says **what** is to be achieved and **why**, and leaves the **how** to the subordinate. The order contains an **intent**, a **task**, the means and the limits. Inside those limits the subordinate decides, and is expected to act on their own when the situation changes, in the spirit of the intent. The reason is friction: in war no plan fits for long, and the person on the spot knows more and can act sooner than the headquarters. Its opposite is **detailed command** (*Befehlstaktik*), which also prescribes the how. Mission command is not a technique for writing orders. It works only where there is **trust**, **common training** and a **shared picture of the situation**, and it fails without them. It is the Swiss Army's method of command at every level.

## Definition

> [!source] Schweizer Armee, *Weisspapier Führung* (2023)
> «Führen mit Auftrag, meist als Auftragstaktik bezeichnet, ist das wesentliche Prinzip der Führung der Schweizer Armee in allen Lagen.»

The Swiss command regulation FSO 17 defines *Auftragstaktik* as the army's method of command, usable at every level, and describes it as leading by setting objectives (*Führen durch Zielvorgabe*) [@schweizerarmee2017fso]. The white paper on leadership adds what marks it: **minimal prescriptions of substance** by the superior, which leads to **decentralised decisions** and the **greatest possible involvement of subordinates** [@hofstetter2023thesen].

The US Army's definition names the same parts: mission command is the approach to command and control that "empowers subordinate decision making and decentralized execution appropriate to the situation" [@usarmy2019mission].

> [!cloze] In mission command the superior states ==what== is to be achieved and ==why==; the subordinate decides ==how==.

**In my words:** I tell you what I want to achieve and why, what you have to do for it, what you get, and what you must not do. The rest is yours. If things change and you cannot reach me, do what I would want, not what I said.

## Key Points

- **The intent is the core.** The intent (*Absicht*) is the purpose behind the task: what the superior wants to achieve. A task can become pointless when the situation changes. The intent stays valid, so it tells the subordinate what to do when the task no longer fits.
- **Freedom of action is bounded.** Mission command is not "do as you like". The intent, the task, the limits and the means given form a frame. Freedom of action (*Handlungsfreiheit*) exists inside it.
- **It is a duty, not only a right.** A subordinate who waits for orders while the situation changes has failed just as much as one who disobeys. Acting on one's own in the spirit of the intent is expected. The US Army calls this *disciplined initiative* [@usarmy2019mission].
- **The superior still commands.** The white paper is clear that command responsibility (*Führungsverantwortung*) always lies with the military superior [@hofstetter2023thesen]. Delegating the how does not delegate the responsibility. The superior follows the action and steps in when the frame is left.
- **As much explicit as necessary, as much implicit as possible.** The white paper ties mission command to the [[OODA Loop]]: a minimum is decided explicitly and a maximum implicitly, inside the subordinates' freedom of action. Units are synchronised through a **shared picture of the situation** (*Lageauffassung*), not through instructions.
- **Two advantages follow.** *Freedom of action*, because a shared picture restricts less than explicit decisions. *Speed*, because a picture can take in changes at once, while decisions take time. Together they give agility [@hofstetter2023thesen].
- **The temptation runs the other way.** Central bodies tend to restrict subordinates more than necessary. The white paper says such restrictions are today "omnipresent" in orders, regulations and directives, and that this endangers the mission.

### What a mission-type order contains

| Part | Question it answers | Left out |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | What is going on, as far as I know? | |
| **Intent** | What do I want to achieve, and why? | |
| **Task** | What do you have to do for it? | *how* to do it |
| Limits | What must you do or not do in any case? | everything not needed |
| Means | What do you get, and who supports you? | |

Swiss orders follow five points: orientation, intent, tasks, special instructions, locations (*Orientierung, Absicht, Aufträge, besondere Anordnungen, Standorte*). The intent has a paragraph of its own, which is mission command built into the form of the order.

### Preconditions

Mission command is a culture before it is a method. It needs:

1. **Trust in both directions.** The superior trusts that the subordinate can and will act in the sense of the intent. The subordinate trusts that honest mistakes are backed. The white paper calls trust indispensable for *Auftragstaktik* and names what destroys it: frequent changes of personnel, and discrimination of any kind [@hofstetter2023thesen].
2. **Competence.** Freedom is worth only what the subordinate can do with it. More freedom of action means harder questions for the subordinate.
3. **A common way of thinking.** Shared [[Doctrine (Swiss Armed Forces)|doctrine]], training and language make it likely that two officers who cannot talk to each other reach compatible decisions.
4. **Tolerance of mistakes and the will to learn.** Whoever punishes every error gets subordinates who wait for orders.
5. **A clear intent.** A superior who does not know what they want cannot lead by intent.

The US Army lists seven principles that cover the same ground: competence, mutual trust, shared understanding, commander's intent, mission orders, disciplined initiative and risk acceptance [@usarmy2019mission].

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Prussia after 1806.** Napoleon destroyed the Prussian army at Jena and Auerstedt. The reformers around Scharnhorst and Gneisenau concluded that an army of obedient executors could not match one that acted fast. They built an educated officer corps and a general staff.
2. **Moltke the Elder.** As Chief of the General Staff (1857–88), Helmuth von Moltke commanded armies that were too large and too far apart to control in detail. His principle: no plan of operations reaches with certainty beyond the first meeting with the enemy's main force, so an order should contain everything the subordinate cannot decide for himself, and nothing more [@bungay2011art].
3. **Into the regulations (1888).** The Prussian infantry drill regulations of 1888 made the principle binding. The word *Auftragstaktik* appeared in the debate that followed, and it is said to have been coined by its critics, who preferred fixed forms (*Normaltaktik*) [@shamir2011transforming].
4. **German armies, 1914–45.** The principle shaped the infiltration tactics of 1918 and the regulation *Truppenführung* of 1933. Van Creveld explains much of the German army's tactical performance by it [@vancreveld1985command].
5. **Adoption abroad (from the 1980s).** After Vietnam, US reformers looked for the source of that performance. [[John Boyd]] used *Auftragstaktik*, *Schwerpunkt* and *Fingerspitzengefühl* in his briefings. The US Marine Corps wrote mission tactics into *Warfighting* [@usmc1989warfighting], the British Army adopted mission command at the end of the 1980s, and the US Army now defines it in ADP 6-0 [@usarmy2019mission].
6. **Today.** The Bundeswehr speaks of *Führen mit Auftrag*. In the Swiss Army it is the method of command in FSO 17 and, in the [[Command, Leadership, Management|CLM model]], the defining approach of the Command aspect [@schweizerarmee2017fso; @hofstetter2023thesen].
7. **Into business.** Bungay translated the idea for companies as *directed opportunism* and leading by intent [@bungay2011art].

### Why it works: Bungay's three gaps

Bungay reads Moltke as an answer to **friction** in Clausewitz's sense. Friction opens three gaps [@bungay2011art]:

| Gap | Between | Usual reaction | Moltke's answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Knowledge gap** | what we would like to know and what we know | collect more information | do not plan beyond what can be foreseen |
| **Alignment gap** | what we want people to do and what they do | give more detailed instructions | pass on the intent, and have it briefed back |
| **Effects gap** | what we expect our actions to achieve and what they achieve | add more controls | give freedom to adjust inside the limits |

The usual reactions make things worse: more information slows the decision, more detail hides the purpose, more control removes the initiative that was needed.

### The opposite pole: Taylor

Scientific Management is detailed command worked out as a system [@taylor1911principles]. Taylor's sentence: in the past the man has been first, in the future the system must be first.

- **The method.** Observe the best workers, split the task into elements, time each element, rebuild the one best way, then train and pay by output.
- **Planning is separated from doing.** A planning department sets standards, time norms, instruction cards and schedules. The shop floor carries them out as written and sends back output data. Authority rests on knowledge of the method, and the system is designed to need as little of the worker's own judgement as possible.
- **Older than Taylor.** Armies broke drill into numbered movements around 1600 (Maurice of Nassau), long before factories standardised work.
- **The army as test case.** In 1909 US Army Ordnance invited Taylor's methods. In 1911 a stopwatch time study at Watertown Arsenal led to a strike of the moulders, a committee of the House of Representatives investigated the "Taylor system", and from 1915 Congress restricted time study in government arsenals. The resistance came from skilled workers who lost control over how they worked.
- **The critique:** craft knowledge moves from the worker to the planning office (deskilling), the stopwatch becomes an instrument of authority, and the social side of work disappears from view.
- **Why it matters here.** Detailed orders, staff planning and time norms raise the same question as the planning department: how far should planning be separated from execution? Mission command answers "as little as possible". Time studies, standard procedures and planning are still everywhere, in armies too, and for drill and weapons handling they are right.

### An early case: Nelson at Trafalgar (1805)

> [!source] Horatio Nelson, memorandum to his captains, HMS *Victory* off Cádiz, 9 October 1805
> "In case Signals can neither be seen or perfectly understood, no Captain can do very wrong if he places his Ship alongside that of an Enemy."

The memorandum has the parts of a mission-type order, eighty years before the word [@nicolas1846dispatches]:

| Element of mission command | At Trafalgar |
|---|---|
| A plan with roles, shared in advance | Two columns were to cut the enemy line. The plan was written down and shared weeks before the battle |
| Intent that holds when control fails | The sentence above: if signals fail, close with the enemy |
| Freedom of action | Collingwood, who led the second column, was left the "entire management" of his line |
| Trust and a common way of thinking | Dinners with his captains, the "band of brothers" |
| Competence | The seamanship, gunnery and morale of the Royal Navy made the risky attack possible |

The lecture uses the case for leader behaviour: the written plan is *initiating structure*, the dinners are *consideration*. Reading it as mission command is my own, and the label is an anachronism.

## Application

- **Giving an order.** State the intent in one or two sentences that a subordinate can repeat under stress. Give tasks, not methods. Name the few limits that really apply. Then have it briefed back: the subordinate says in their own words what they will do and why.
- **Receiving an order.** Ask what the superior, and the level above, want to achieve. Ask what has changed since the order was written. Then decide the how.
- **When the situation changes.** If the task still serves the intent, carry on. If it no longer does and the superior can be reached, report and propose. If the superior cannot be reached, act in the spirit of the intent and report as soon as possible.
- **Manoeuvre warfare.** A fleeting weakness is gone before a headquarters can order its use. So [[Attrition vs Manoeuvre Warfare|manoeuvre]] depends on mission command, and attrition can live with detailed command.
- **Outside the army.** A project lead who says "the client must be able to test on Friday, the budget is fixed, the rest is up to you" leads by intent. One who prescribes each step leads by detailed command.

> [!assessment] Mission command in a militia army
> Switzerland has two things that favour mission command and one that works against it. In its favour: militia soldiers bring civilian competence and are used to deciding for themselves, and a decentralised state is used to subsidiarity. Against it: trust and a common way of thinking grow in shared service, and militia units serve together for only a few weeks a year. The white paper's own complaint, that orders and regulations restrict far more than necessary, suggests that the principle is more firmly held in the regulations than in practice. This is my own reading.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Proclaimed more often than practised.** Shamir shows that the US, British and Israeli armies adopted the doctrine but kept habits that contradict it: careers that punish mistakes, detailed orders, control from above. A culture cannot be imported with a regulation [@shamir2011transforming].
> - **Technology invites micromanagement.** A headquarters that sees every vehicle on a screen is tempted to steer it. The better the picture at the top, the stronger the pull towards detailed command.
> - **Some tasks need detailed command.** Where actions must be exactly coordinated or mistakes cannot be corrected, the how is prescribed: airspace control, fire support coordination, nuclear weapons, rules of engagement. Mission command and detailed command are ends of a scale, and the right point depends on the task.
> - **It can produce incoherence.** Subordinates who each act on their own reading of the intent can pull in different directions, or commit the force to something the superior never wanted. German commanders in 1914 are a standard example of initiative that wrecked the plan.
> - **Tactical freedom, strategic blindness.** The German armies that practised it best lost both world wars. Excellence in the how says nothing about whether the what and the why are sound.
> - **Rules become ends.** Merton's point about bureaucracy applies to armies in peace: rules written for foreseen cases are followed for their own sake and block adaptation to the case nobody foresaw. That is the mechanism behind the white paper's complaint about over-restriction.
> - **It makes the person matter more.** In a **strong situation** (drill, weapons handling by standard procedure, barracks routine) clear rules make behaviour uniform. In a **weak situation** (a night patrol after contact is lost and the plan no longer fits) individual differences show. Mission command creates weak situations on purpose, so it stands or falls with the selection and training of the people who fill them. The distinction is from the lecture, the conclusion is mine.
> - **Political sensitivity.** In peace support or in aid to the civil authorities, the act of one soldier can have political effects. That argues for tighter limits, and it collides with the freedom the method requires.

## Key Connections

- [[OODA Loop]]: mission command is how an organisation uses *implicit guidance and control*, acting from a shared orientation without waiting for explicit decisions
- [[Command, Leadership, Management]]: it is the defining approach of the Command aspect, and it makes demands on Leadership (trust) and Management (a shared picture)
- [[Doctrine (Swiss Armed Forces)]]: shared doctrine gives the common way of thinking that mission command needs, and over-detailed doctrine destroys it
- [[Attrition vs Manoeuvre Warfare]]: manoeuvre depends on mission command to use fleeting weaknesses, attrition goes with detailed command
- [[John Boyd]]: he brought *Auftragstaktik* into US thinking as part of his organic design for command and control

- [[Course of Action]]: the planning procedure produces the intent and the tasks that a mission-type order passes on
- [[Attribution]]: giving freedom of action means judging subordinates by their situation and intent, not only by the result

## Self-Test: Mission Command

> [!qard]- 1. Define mission command in one sentence.
> The superior states what is to be achieved and why, with means and limits. The subordinate decides how, and acts on their own in the spirit of the intent when the situation changes.

> [!qard]- 2. What is the opposite of *Auftragstaktik*, and what is the difference?
> *Befehlstaktik*, detailed command. It prescribes the how as well as the what. It gives tight coordination but needs a superior who knows the local situation and can be reached in time.

> [!qard]- 3. Why is the intent more important than the task?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> A task fits one situation and can become pointless when it changes. The intent, the purpose behind the task, stays valid. It tells the subordinate what to do when the task no longer fits and nobody can be asked.

> [!qard]- 4. Name the preconditions of mission command.
> Trust in both directions, competence of the subordinates, a common way of thinking (doctrine, training, language), tolerance of mistakes, and a clear intent.

> [!qard]- 5. Which two advantages does the Swiss white paper name, and where do they come from?
> Freedom of action and speed. Units are synchronised through a shared picture of the situation, which restricts less than explicit decisions and takes in changes faster. Together they give agility.

> [!qard]- 6. Where does *Auftragstaktik* come from?
> From Prussia: the reforms after the defeat of 1806, Moltke the Elder's practice of command by directive, and the infantry regulations of 1888. The US and British armies adopted it in the 1980s.

> [!qard]- 7. Name Bungay's three gaps and the usual, wrong reaction to each.
> Knowledge gap: more information. Alignment gap: more detailed instructions. Effects gap: more controls. Each reaction slows the organisation and removes the initiative it needs.

> [!qard]- 8. Apply: your platoon is to hold a bridge "so that the battalion can cross at 0600". At 0400 you learn that the battalion will cross at a ford 2 km north. Radio is down. What do you do?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> The task (hold the bridge) no longer serves the intent (let the battalion cross). Act on the intent: secure the crossing at the ford, with what the limits allow, and report as soon as contact is back.

> [!qard]- 9. Explain with the OODA loop why mission command makes an organisation faster.
> <!-- qard-write -->
> With a shared orientation, subordinates act through implicit guidance and control: from orientation directly to action, without waiting for an explicit decision from above. The organisation's loop is no longer as slow as its hierarchy.

> [!qard]- 10. Compare: in which situations is detailed command the better choice?
> Where actions must be exactly coordinated or errors cannot be corrected (airspace, fire support, nuclear weapons, rules of engagement), and where trust or competence are missing, for example in a newly formed unit.

> [!qard]- 11. Limit: why do armies that proclaim mission command often not practise it?
> It is a culture, not a regulation. Careers that punish mistakes, detailed orders and technology that lets the top see everything all pull towards control (Shamir).

> [!qard]- 12. Does mission command relieve the superior of responsibility?
> No. Command responsibility always lies with the military superior. Only the how is delegated. The superior follows the action and steps in when the frame of the intent is left.

> [!qard]- 13. Compare: what does Scientific Management do with planning and doing, and how does mission command differ?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> Taylor separates them: a planning department finds the one best way and the shop floor executes as written, with as little own judgement as possible. Mission command joins them: the superior gives intent and task, the subordinate plans the how.

> [!qard]- 14. Apply: which elements of mission command does Nelson's memorandum of 9 October 1805 show?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> A plan shared in advance, an intent that holds when signals fail (place your ship alongside an enemy), freedom of action for Collingwood's column, and trust built with his captains beforehand.
