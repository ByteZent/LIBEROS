> [!bluf]
> **Attrition** seeks victory by destroying the enemy's physical means faster than he can replace them: **strength against strength**. **Manoeuvre** seeks victory by breaking the enemy's **cohesion** and will through tempo, surprise and position: **strength against weakness**. Attrition is predictable but costly; manoeuvre is cheaper when it works but risky and dependent on knowing the enemy well. They are **not rival philosophies to pick from** but ends of a spectrum, and the choice depends on the **perceived force ratio**. Hofstetter, Borioli and Flemming argue that forces choose manoeuvre when they see the enemy as weaker, attrition when equal and guerrilla when stronger. A war freezes into attrition when neither side has an edge, and only an **innovation** can unfreeze it.

## Definition

> [!source] British Army, *Design for Military Operations* (1996)
> Manoeuvre warfare "aims to apply strength … against weakness, in contrast to attrition where strength tends to be applied against strength."

**Luttwak's version** is the clearest analytical one. Attrition treats the enemy as **a set of targets**: success comes from efficient, cumulative destruction, so results are roughly proportional to effort, and the approach is predictable and low-risk, but expensive. **Relational manoeuvre** does not try to destroy the enemy's strength, but to **neutralise it**: find a weakness and apply selective force there. Results can be far out of proportion to effort, but the approach is risky, because it depends on reading the enemy correctly, and a misjudged weakness can turn into a disaster [@luttwak1980operational].

**In my words:** attrition wins by *adding up* damage; manoeuvre wins by *multiplying* it, making the enemy's system fall apart before his forces are used up.

| | **Attrition** | **Manoeuvre** |
|---|---|---|
| Target | the enemy's physical means (people, equipment, stocks) | the enemy's cohesion, command and will |
| Logic | strength against strength | strength against weakness |
| Results | proportional to effort; cumulative | disproportionate; sudden collapse |
| Key factors | mass, firepower, industry, endurance | tempo, surprise, deception, focus (*Schwerpunkt*) |
| Risk | low risk per action, high total cost | high risk, lower cost if it works |
| Nature | static, methodical | dynamic, opportunistic |
| Command | **detailed command**: central control, synchronisation | **mission command**: intent, initiative, decentralisation |
| Measure of success | attrition ratios, ground held | enemy's loss of coherence |

## Key Points

- **Manoeuvre is not only movement.** It is about **position** in a broad sense: physical, but also in time and in the enemy's mind. A force can move a lot and still fight attrition, or hold still and fight manoeuvre (e.g. by an ambush that paralyses an enemy's command).
- **The target of manoeuvre is the enemy's orientation.** In [[OODA Loop|Boyd's]] terms, manoeuvre gets *inside* the enemy's loop, so he keeps reacting to a situation that has already changed until his picture of reality collapses [@boyd2018discourse].
- **Manoeuvre needs mission command.** Exploiting fleeting weakness requires subordinates who act on intent without waiting for orders. That is why manoeuvre doctrine and mission command came together, e.g. in the US Marine Corps' *Warfighting* [@usmc1989warfighting].
- **Attrition is not stupid.** It is the rational choice when neither side has a decisive edge, when the enemy has no exploitable weakness, or when you have more resources and time than he does.
- **Both are always present.** Every manoeuvre involves fighting and losses; every attrition campaign looks for local weaknesses. The question is what the *main* logic is.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Ancient roots.** Sun Tzu already tied the choice of approach to the **force ratio**: surround with overwhelming superiority, attack with a clear advantage, divide the enemy with a modest one, engage when equal, and evade when weaker [@suntzu1963art, ch. 3].
2. **First World War.** The Western Front became the model of attrition; Verdun (1916) was planned explicitly to bleed the French army. The 1918 German infiltration tactics were an attempt to break out of it.
3. **Interwar and Second World War.** Liddell Hart's *indirect approach*, Soviet *deep battle* and the German campaign of 1940 became the reference cases for manoeuvre. The image of a planned German *Blitzkrieg* doctrine is partly a later myth; the 1940 success owed much to improvisation and French errors.
4. **The US manoeuvre debate (1970s–80s).** After Vietnam, reformers around [[John Boyd]] and William Lind argued that US doctrine had become attritional. Their ideas shaped AirLand Battle (1982) and the Marine Corps' *Warfighting* (1989) [@lind1985maneuver; @usmc1989warfighting]. The British Army adopted the *manoeuvrist approach* in its doctrine in 1989 [@ukarmy1996design].
5. **Ukraine since 2022.** After the mobile phase of 2022, the front largely froze. The failed 2023 Ukrainian counter-offensive and dense drone surveillance led many to ask whether **manoeuvre is dead** on a transparent battlefield, which was the question of the 2024 Theresan Military Academic Forum [@hofstetter2024manoeuvre].

## Application

### The spectrum model (Hofstetter, Borioli & Flemming, 2024)

![[attrition-manoeuvre-spectrum.svg]]

The authors turn the dichotomy into a spectrum and a set of testable claims. They assume two rational actors who decide on the basis of **perceptions** [@hofstetter2024manoeuvre].

- **Thesis I.** The choice depends on how the enemy is **perceived**: as weaker → manoeuvre; as equal → attrition; as stronger → guerrilla.
- **Corollary Ia.** Guerrilla and manoeuvre are **dynamic**; attrition is **static**.
- **Corollary Ib.** Dynamic warfare goes with **mission command**, attrition with **detailed command**.
- **Shifts.** Moving right: *intensifying* (guerrilla → attrition) and *unfreezing* (attrition → manoeuvre). Moving left: *freezing* (manoeuvre → attrition) and *hiding* (attrition → guerrilla).
- **Thesis II.** Armed forces try to move **as far right as they can**, to exploit a competitive advantage.
- **Corollary II.** **Innovation** creates the opportunities that make unfreezing possible.
- **Thesis III.** An innovation that adds combat value and is rare gives only a **temporary** advantage. To last, it must be hard to imitate and supported by the organisation; this is the [[VRIO|CRIO]] logic.

**Why *perceived* strength matters:** decisions are made on the picture in the commander's head, not on the true balance. An army that overrates the enemy freezes into attrition when it could manoeuvre; one that underrates him tries to manoeuvre and gets destroyed. This links the model back to *orientation* in the [[OODA Loop]].

> [!assessment] Reading Ukraine through the model
> Both sides came to see each other as roughly equal, and the war froze into attrition. Each new capability (Western artillery, drones, electronic warfare, glide bombs) gave one side a local edge until the other copied or countered it, so the front never unfroze. That is Thesis III in practice: the innovations were valuable and briefly rare, but easy to imitate. *(My application, not the authors'.)*

### Swiss relevance

The Swiss debate between a **mobile** school and a **static** school of defence (see [[Doctrine (Swiss Armed Forces)]]) is this debate in national form. A small militia army defending its own terrain has tended towards the static, attritional pole: use terrain and obstacles to wear down a stronger attacker and gain time.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **A false dichotomy.** Most historians and practitioners see the two as complementary. Attrition often creates the conditions for manoeuvre, and manoeuvre is judged by its effect on the enemy's means as well as his mind.
> - **Manoeuvre as ideology.** Critics argue that the 1980s manoeuvre school idealised German practice, treated attrition as intellectually inferior, and promised cheap victories that rarely come.
> - **Force employment matters more than the label.** Biddle shows that since 1918 outcomes depend less on numbers or technology than on the **modern system** of force employment: cover, concealment, dispersion, combined arms and depth. That system is neither pure attrition nor pure manoeuvre [@biddle2004military].
> - **Is the spectrum too neat?** The Hofstetter model assumes two rational actors and one dimension (perceived strength). Real choices also depend on politics, casualty tolerance, time pressure and alliance constraints. The authors state these limits themselves.

## Self-Test

> [!question]- 1. Define attrition and manoeuvre in one sentence each.
> **Attrition:** victory by destroying the enemy's physical means faster than he can replace them, strength against strength. **Manoeuvre:** victory by breaking the enemy's cohesion and will through tempo, surprise and position, strength against weakness.

> [!question]- 2. Why does manoeuvre go with mission command and attrition with detailed command?
> Manoeuvre exploits short-lived weaknesses, so subordinates must act on intent without waiting. Attrition is methodical and depends on synchronising mass and fire, which favours central control.

> [!question]- 3. State Thesis I with both corollaries, and explain why it uses *perceived* strength.
> Enemy seen as weaker → manoeuvre; equal → attrition; stronger → guerrilla. Guerrilla and manoeuvre are dynamic and use mission command; attrition is static and uses detailed command. Decisions rest on the commander's picture of the balance, which can be wrong, not on the true balance.

> [!question]- 4. What does it take to unfreeze an attritional war, according to Hofstetter et al.?
> An innovation that gives a competitive advantage (Corollary II). For the advantage to last, it must be not only valuable and rare but also hard to imitate and organisationally supported (Thesis III, CRIO).

> [!question]- 5. Give the strongest objection to the attrition–manoeuvre dichotomy.
> It is a false choice: both are always present, and outcomes depend on how forces are employed (Biddle's modern system), not on which label a doctrine uses.
