> [!bluf]
> Each major school of psychology rests on a **Menschenbild**, an image of what a human being is and what primarily drives behaviour:
> - **Depth psychology:** unconscious conflicts and drives.
> - **Behaviourism:** learning from the environment and its consequences.
> - **Cognitive psychology:** how people process and interpret information.
> - **Humanistic psychology:** free will and the striving to grow.
> - **Biological psychology:** body, brain, hormones and genes.
>
> They are **perspectives, not types of people**. Like pieces of a mosaic, they overlap: the same behaviour is usually shaped by several at once. For a military leader, each school suggests different questions and different levers (training, framing, meaning, rest), and taking only one view leads to predictable mistakes.

## Definition

A **Menschenbild** (literally "image of the human") is the set of basic assumptions a theory makes about human nature: what drives people, how free they are, how far they can change, and what should be studied to understand them. These assumptions are usually not tested themselves; they decide **which questions a school asks** and **which evidence it accepts**.

**In my words:** a Menschenbild is the lens a psychologist looks through before looking at anything. Change the lens and the same soldier, doing the same thing, becomes a different problem.

## Key Points

| School | What mainly explains behaviour? | Image of the human | Key terms | Main figures |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Depth psychology** (*Tiefenpsychologie*) | unconscious processes, inner conflicts, early experience | driven by forces the person does not see | unconscious; id, ego, superego; drives; defence mechanisms | Freud, Adler, Jung |
| **Behaviourism** (*Behaviorismus*) | environment, learning, consequences | shaped by what has been reinforced; the mind as a *black box* | stimulus–response; classical and operant conditioning; reinforcement and punishment | Pavlov, Watson, Skinner |
| **Cognitive psychology** (*Kognitivismus*) | how information is perceived, processed and evaluated | an active information processor who interprets | perception, attention, memory, schemas, appraisal | Neisser, Beck, Lazarus |
| **Humanistic psychology** (*Humanistische Psychologie*) | subjective experience, free choice, need for growth | free, responsible, striving to realise potential | self-actualisation, self-concept, hierarchy of needs | Rogers, Maslow |
| **Biological psychology** (*Biologische Psychologie*) | nervous system, hormones, genes, evolution | a biological organism whose mind rests on the body | neurons, neurotransmitters, stress hormones, heritability | Cannon, Selye, today neuroscience |

### Depth psychology

Freud's structural model has three instances [@freud1923ich]:

- **Id** (*Es*): drives and wishes, seeking immediate satisfaction.
- **Superego** (*Über-Ich*): internalised norms and conscience.
- **Ego** (*Ich*): mediates between id, superego and reality.

When the conflict between them becomes too strong, the ego protects itself with **defence mechanisms** such as repression, projection, denial or rationalisation. Behaviour then has reasons the person is not aware of.

**Military angle:** Freud's *Massenpsychologie und Ich-Analyse* uses the army as a main example of an "artificial mass". It is held together by emotional ties to the leader and among comrades; if those ties break, **panic** follows even if the physical danger has not grown [@freud1921massenpsychologie]. Modern research on **unit cohesion** asks the same question with other methods.

### Behaviourism

Behaviourism studies only what can be observed: stimulus and response. The mind is treated as a **black box** [@watson1913psychology].

- **Classical conditioning** (Pavlov): a neutral stimulus becomes linked to a reflex (e.g. a sound to fear).
- **Operant conditioning** (Skinner): behaviour is shaped by its **consequences** [@skinner1953science].

| | Something is **added** | Something is **removed** |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviour **increases** | positive reinforcement (praise, leave) | negative reinforcement (an unpleasant duty is dropped) |
| Behaviour **decreases** | positive punishment (extra duty) | negative punishment (leave cancelled) |

**Negative reinforcement is not punishment.** "Negative" means something is *taken away*; "reinforcement" means the behaviour becomes *more* likely.

The **ABC scheme** analyses behaviour in three parts:
- **A**ntecedent (what came before)
- **B**ehaviour
- **C**onsequence (what followed).

To change behaviour, change the antecedent or the consequence.

**Military angle:** drill and realistic training are applied behaviourism: they make the right response automatic under stress. Replacing bullseye targets with man-shaped pop-up targets after the Second World War was meant to condition soldiers to fire in combat [@grossman1995killing]. The claim this rested on, that most soldiers in that war did not fire, is disputed.

> [!warning] Two different ABCs
> Cognitive therapy has another ABC (Ellis): **A**ctivating event → **B**elief → **C**onsequence. Here the *belief* about an event, not the event itself, produces the emotional reaction. Same letters, opposite idea: the behaviourist ABC leaves the mind out, the cognitive ABC puts it at the centre.

### Cognitive psychology

The "cognitive turn" of the 1960s opened the black box. People are seen as active processors who **perceive, select, interpret and remember** information through **schemas** [@neisser1967cognitive]. The same situation can lead to different behaviour because it is *interpreted* differently.

**Military angle:** stress is a clear case. In Lazarus's model, a situation causes stress only if it is **appraised** as a threat (*primary appraisal*) and one's resources as insufficient (*secondary appraisal*) [@lazarus1984stress]. Training changes both: it makes situations familiar and builds confidence in one's resources. Situational awareness and the *orient* step of the [[OODA Loop]] are cognitive concepts.

### Humanistic psychology

A "third force" against both psychoanalysis and behaviourism. People are seen as **free, responsible and oriented towards growth**. Rogers stressed the **self-concept** and the conditions for growth: acceptance, empathy and genuineness [@rogers1961becoming]. Maslow's **hierarchy of needs** runs from physiological needs through safety, belonging and esteem to **self-actualisation** [@maslow1943theory].

**Military angle:** leadership that relies on **meaning, trust and responsibility** (e.g. mission command) implicitly assumes a humanistic image of the soldier. Maslow's lower levels are also a practical reminder: sleep, food and safety come first, and a soldier who lacks them will not be moved by appeals to purpose.

### Biological psychology

Experience and behaviour are explained through their **physical basis**: the nervous system, neurotransmitters, hormones and genes. Cannon described the **fight-or-flight** response; Selye described how long-term stress wears the body down.

**Military angle:** the physiology of stress (heart rate, adrenaline, cortisol) explains why fine motor skills and complex thinking break down under acute threat, why **sleep deprivation** damages judgement long before it is felt, and why combat trauma has lasting bodily effects. It is the basis for selection, work–rest planning and recovery.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Depth psychology (from c. 1900):** Freud's psychoanalysis, then Adler and Jung, who broke with him.
2. **Behaviourism (from 1913):** Watson's manifesto rejects introspection; psychology should study only observable behaviour [@watson1913psychology]. Skinner extends it from the 1930s.
3. **Humanistic psychology (1950s–60s):** a reaction against the determinism of both.
4. **Cognitive turn (1960s):** computers provide the model of the mind as information processor [@neisser1967cognitive].
5. **Biological and neuroscientific psychology (from the 1990s):** imaging and genetics make the biological perspective the fastest-growing one.
6. **Integration:** today most explanations combine perspectives, e.g. in the **biopsychosocial model**, which treats illness and behaviour as the product of biological, psychological and social factors together [@engel1977need].

## Application

**One case, five views.** *A soldier who used to volunteer for tasks has stopped doing so since a new platoon leader took over.* *(Invented example.)*

| Perspective | Possible explanation | What a leader could do |
|---|---|---|
| **Depth** | The new leader reminds him of an authority figure he rejects; the resistance is not conscious. | Hard for a leader to address; recognise that the reaction may not be about the leader personally. |
| **Behaviourist** | Volunteering used to be praised; now it only leads to more work (punishment), and nothing rewards it. | Change the consequences: recognise volunteers, share extra duties fairly. |
| **Cognitive** | He interprets the new leader's corrections as distrust ("he thinks I'm not good enough"). | Clarify expectations; give explicit feedback that changes the interpretation. |
| **Humanistic** | Under the new leader, tasks are tightly prescribed; he no longer experiences responsibility or growth. | Give intent and freedom of action; let him own a task. |
| **Biological** | Accumulated sleep debt and physical strain since the change in training rhythm. | Check rest and load before looking for psychological causes. |

The explanations are **not exclusive**. A good diagnosis checks the cheap, observable ones first (sleep, consequences) and keeps the others in mind.

### Do not confuse: Schein's organisational Menschenbilder

Organisational psychology has a separate, well-known typology of **images of the employee** by Schein: the *rational-economic* man (motivated by money), the *social* man (by belonging), the *self-actualising* man (by growth) and the *complex* man (it depends on the person and situation) [@schein1965organizational]. It overlaps with the schools above (e.g. the self-actualising man is humanistic), but it is a model of motivation at work, not a list of schools of psychology.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **Depth psychology is hard to falsify.** Popper's classic critique: psychoanalysis can explain any behaviour *and* its opposite (e.g. through reaction formation), so no observation can refute it. A theory that explains everything tests nothing [@popper1963conjectures].
> - **Behaviourism ignores the mind.** It struggles with language, insight and learning without reinforcement. Its methods also raise ethical questions when used to condition soldiers to kill.
> - **Cognitive psychology can be too "cold".** It models people as computers and underplays emotion, motivation and the body.
> - **Humanistic psychology is vague.** Concepts like self-actualisation are hard to measure; Maslow's strict hierarchy has little empirical support.
> - **Biological psychology risks reductionism.** Showing *where* something happens in the brain does not explain *why* a person acts; meaning and context get lost.
> - **Each school is a model.** Each reduces the human to what it can study, for its own purpose (see [[General Model Theory]]). Trouble begins when one lens is taken for the whole picture.

## Self-Test

> [!question]- 1. Name the five schools and what each sees as the main driver of behaviour.
> Depth psychology: unconscious conflicts. Behaviourism: learning and consequences from the environment. Cognitive: information processing and interpretation. Humanistic: free will and growth. Biological: brain, hormones, genes.

> [!question]- 2. Explain negative reinforcement and positive punishment with a military example.
> **Negative reinforcement:** something unpleasant is removed so the behaviour increases (a platoon that passes inspection is excused from the evening cleaning). **Positive punishment:** something unpleasant is added so the behaviour decreases (extra guard duty for being late).

> [!question]- 3. What is the ABC scheme, and how does it differ from Ellis's ABC?
> Behaviourist: Antecedent → Behaviour → Consequence; change A or C to change B. Ellis (cognitive): Activating event → Belief → Consequence; the belief about the event produces the reaction. One leaves the mind out, the other centres on it.

> [!question]- 4. Why is depth psychology criticised as unfalsifiable?
> It can explain any outcome and its opposite (through defence mechanisms), so no observation can prove it wrong (Popper). A scientific theory must forbid some outcomes.

> [!question]- 5. What does the mosaic image mean?
> The schools are perspectives, not types of people. They overlap, and the same behaviour is usually shaped by several factors at once. Good explanations combine perspectives.
