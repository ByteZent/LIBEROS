> [!bluf]
> The OODA loop (**Observe – Orient – Decide – Act**) is [[John Boyd]]'s model of how people and organisations adapt in competition. Its core is **not speed but orientation**: the mental model through which observations are interpreted, which also shapes what we observe, decide and do. The advantage goes to whoever **adapts faster *relative* to the opponent**: someone who orients better and acts coherently forces the opponent to keep reacting to situations that have already changed, until the opponent's picture of reality falls apart. It is a model of **learning under uncertainty**, not a four-step checklist.
## Definition

> [!source] Boyd, "Organic Design for Command and Control" (1987)
> "Orientation is the Schwerpunkt. It shapes the way we interact with the environment."

**Boyd's claim, paraphrased:** to win, operate at a faster tempo or rhythm than the adversary. Better still, get *inside* his observation–orientation–decision–action loop, so that his actions keep lagging behind a situation that has already changed.

**In my words:** everyone runs a loop of noticing, making sense, choosing and acting. The side whose sense-making stays closer to reality, and which can change its behaviour coherently, drags the other side into responding to a world that no longer exists.

## Key Points

- **Observe** (*Beobachten*): take in outside information, unfolding circumstances, and the effects of your own actions. Observation is **selective**: orientation decides what you notice.
- **Orient** (*Orientieren*): combine new information with **cultural traditions, genetic heritage, previous experience** through **analysis and synthesis** into a mental picture. This is the **hinge** of the whole model. Two actors can observe the same signal and reach opposite conclusions.
- **Decide** (*Entscheiden*): choose a course of action. Boyd calls it a **hypothesis**, a provisional bet on what will work, not a final truth.
- **Act** (*Handeln*): the **test** of the hypothesis. Action changes the situation and produces new observations, so acting is also a way of learning.
- **It is not a circle.** Boyd's diagram has multiple **feedback** loops (from Decide and Act back to Observe) and **implicit guidance and control**: a well-trained orientation can drive action and observation *directly*, without an explicit decision. This is what makes routines and [[#Mission command and implicit guidance|mission command]] fast.
- **Tempo is relative.** Speed only helps if orientation is sound. A fast loop built on a false picture just produces wrong actions faster.
- **Self-similar across levels:** the same logic applies to a fighter pilot, a battalion staff, a ministry or a grand strategy. Only the time scale, information and responsibility change.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Air combat (1950s–60s):** Boyd, a fighter pilot and instructor, asked why US F-86 pilots in Korea did well against the MiG-15, which was superior on paper. His answer: bubble canopy (better *observation*) and hydraulic controls (faster *transition between manoeuvres*).
2. **Energy-manoeuvrability theory (1960s):** a quantitative theory of fighter performance that shaped the F-15 and F-16.
3. **"Destruction and Creation" (1976):** an epistemological essay drawing on Gödel, Heisenberg and the second law of thermodynamics. Any closed mental model degrades, so we must keep *destroying* and *re-creating* our concepts. This is the philosophical root of *Orient*.
4. **"Patterns of Conflict" (1976–86):** a briefing that surveys military history to argue for **manoeuvre** over attrition: win by disrupting the opponent's ability to orient, not by destroying mass.
5. **"The Essence of Winning and Losing" (1995):** the only diagram of the full loop Boyd drew himself, the one redrawn above.

## Application

### Manoeuvre warfare

Attrition seeks victory by destroying the enemy's physical means. **Manoeuvre** seeks to break the enemy's **cohesion**: through surprise, ambiguity and tempo, it drives his orientation to mismatch reality until his system collapses from within. Boyd's ideas strongly shaped the US Marine Corps' *FMFM 1 Warfighting* (1989). Compare Clausewitz's cohesion-based *centre of gravity*.

### Mission command and implicit guidance

Boyd's *organic design for command and control* borrows German terms. **Einheit** is mutual trust and a shared outlook. **Fingerspitzengefühl** is intuitive feel for the situation. **Auftragstaktik** is mission-type orders: *what* and *why*, not *how*. **Schwerpunkt** is focus of effort. Together they create a **shared orientation**, so subordinates can act through *implicit guidance and control* instead of waiting for explicit decisions. That is how an organisation's loop becomes faster than its hierarchy.

### Leadership practice

Questions a leader can ask:
- **Observe:** what information are we missing, and what are we filtering out?
- **Orient:** what mental picture do we share, and what would prove it wrong?
- **Decide:** is this decision framed as a hypothesis, with a way to test it?
- **Act:** what feedback tells us the effect is (or isn't) what we expected?

## OODA vs Sensemaking

A classic exam comparison. **Define both separately, then connect them through orientation.** Never equate them.

| | **Sensemaking** (Weick) | **OODA** (Boyd) |
|---|---|---|
| Guiding question | *What is going on here?* Which interpretation is plausible? | How do I adapt observation and action faster than the opponent? |
| Focus | Meaning, frames, identity, social construction | Competitive adaptation: orientation, decision, action, feedback |
| Time | Largely **retrospective**: making sense of what happened | Continuous: feedback shapes **future** action |
| Output | A plausible, actionable interpretation | An executed hypothesis and new observations |
| Typical failure | A plausible frame that blinds you to contrary evidence | A mechanical four-step routine, or pure speed logic |
| **Interface** | Sensemaking describes much of what happens *inside* **Orient** | Orient is where OODA depends on good sensemaking |

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **The "faster is better" misreading.** Popular versions reduce OODA to cycling faster. Boyd's emphasis is orientation and adaptation, and speed without sound orientation is harmful (Osinga).
> - **Too general to test?** A model that fits every decision risks explaining everything and predicting nothing.
> - **Naturalistic decision-making** (Klein): experts often go from *recognising* a situation directly to *acting*, without deliberate option comparison. This actually fits Boyd's *implicit guidance and control* better than the simple four-step version does.
> - **Transfer from air combat:** a one-on-one duel with clear feedback is not a coalition campaign, a bureaucracy or a policy process. Loops at higher levels are slower, noisier and politically contested.
> - **Getting "inside" the enemy's loop assumes you can read it.** An adversary with a different culture may not be disoriented by what would disorient you.

## Self-Test: OODA

> [!qard]- 1. Explain the four elements and the key feedback loops.
> - **Observe** gathers information, circumstances and the effects of your own action.
> - **Orient** interprets them through experience, culture, heritage, and analysis and synthesis.
>  - **Decide** selects a hypothesis.
>  - **Act** tests it. Feedback runs from Decide and Act back to Observe. *Implicit guidance and control* runs from Orient directly to Observe and to Act. The loop is continuous and non-linear, not a checklist.

> [!qard]- 2. Why is orientation the central element?
> It determines what is observed, which options are even visible, and how risks are judged. Boyd calls it the *Schwerpunkt*. With a wrong orientation, a faster loop only produces wrong actions faster.

> [!qard]- 3. Why is a fast loop without sound orientation not enough?
> The advantage is **relative adaptability**, not absolute speed. Tempo pays off only if signals are interpreted correctly and actions fit the purpose and the environment. Otherwise it multiplies errors.

> [!qard]- 4. How does manoeuvre warfare use the opponent's OODA loop?
> By surprise, ambiguity and tempo it creates mismatches between the enemy's orientation and reality. He keeps reacting to outdated situations until his cohesion collapses. The target is his *ability to orient*, not his physical mass.

> [!qard]- 5. How do OODA and sensemaking differ, and where do they meet?
> Sensemaking explains how meaning is constructed, often retrospectively and socially. OODA is a competitive cycle of decision and action with feedback. They meet in **Orient**, but neither can be reduced to the other.

> [!qard]- 6. When does implicit guidance enable speed, and when is it risky?
> It enables speed when orientation is shared (training, trust, clear intent, known limits). It becomes risky in novel or ambiguous situations, with weak trust or unclear boundaries. Then explicit decision is needed.

> [!qard]- 7. Apply: a staff issues orders faster than the enemy but keeps being surprised. Diagnose it with OODA.
> The fault is in **Orient**, not in tempo. The shared picture no longer matches reality, so fast decisions are fast errors. Ask what would prove the picture wrong and what is being filtered out.

> [!qard]- 8. Limit: why can "getting inside the enemy's loop" fail against a culturally different adversary?
> It assumes you can read his orientation. What would disorient you may not disorient him. And above the level of a duel, loops are slow, noisy and politically contested.
