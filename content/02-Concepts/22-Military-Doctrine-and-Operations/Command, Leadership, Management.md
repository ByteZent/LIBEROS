> [!bluf]
> **CLM** is the Swiss Armed Forces' model of what leading (*Führung*) consists of. It has three aspects, each named after what it is oriented towards: **Command** towards the **mission** (*Auftrag*), **Leadership** towards the **people** (*Mensch*), and **Management** towards the **organisation**. The model's claim is that the civilian pair "leadership versus management" is incomplete: a superior can treat people well and run flawless processes and still fail, because the decision itself was wrong. Command names that missing third part, the thinking about the task. No aspect replaces another, and good leading is a property of a **team**, not of one person. The army's leadership adopted the model on 27 March 2024 as the basis for all leadership training.

## Definition

> [!source] Schweizer Armee, *Weisspapier Führung* (2023)
> «Erfolgreiche Führung ist daher auftragszentriert, menschenorientiert und organisationsbasiert.»

The white paper defines each aspect in one sentence [@hofstetter2023thesen]:

> [!cloze] Command is the aspect of leading oriented towards the ==mission==, Leadership the aspect oriented towards the ==people==, and Management the aspect oriented towards the ==organisation==.

**In my words:** every leadership problem has three sides. *What do we have to achieve, and how?* is Command. *Who has to achieve it, and will they?* is Leadership. *With what structures and procedures?* is Management. CLM is a checklist for not forgetting one of them.

![[clm-model-swiss-armed-forces.svg]]

The army's own figure, redrawn with its German labels. Read it from the centre outwards: each ring is one layer of the model, named on the dark band (*Ausrichtung*, *Führung*, *Erziehung*, *Ausbildung*, *Etablierung*). The colours are part of the model: Command is green, Leadership red, Management blue.

Note the two meanings of the word. In the model, *Leadership* is one of three aspects. In everyday English, and in the course title, "leadership" means the whole of *Führung*. The army itself calls the whole thing the "Leadership-Modell".

## Key Points

### The three aspects

| | **Command** | **Leadership** | **Management** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oriented towards | mission (*Auftrag*) | people (*Mensch*) | organisation |
| Kind of leading | mission-centred (*auftragszentriert*) | people-oriented (*menschenorientiert*) | organisation-based (*organisationsbasiert*) |
| Challenges | of substance (*inhaltlich*): tactical, operational, military-strategic problems | human: leading oneself, a group, a formation | organisational: planning and following the situation |
| Solutions | conceptual | communal (*gemeinschaftlich*) | institutional |
| Established in (*Etablierung*) | **doctrine** | **culture** | **structures and processes** |
| Education (*Erziehung*) conveys | purpose (*Sinn*) | values (*Werte*) | order (*Ordnung*) |
| Training (*Ausbildung*) builds | sureness in action (*Handlungssicherheit*) | sureness in behaviour (*Verhaltenssicherheit*) | sureness in procedure (*Verfahrenssicherheit*) |
| Central idea | responsibility | trust | the core process, the [[OODA Loop]] |
| Defining approach in the Swiss Army | [[Mission Command\|mission command]] (*Auftragstaktik*) | military ethics | depends on the level of technology |
| Colour in the model | green | red | blue |

All rows are from the white paper and Hofstetter's later account of it [@hofstetter2023thesen; @hofstetter2025mehrwert].

- **Command is specific, Leadership is generic.** Missions differ, so Command competence depends on the trade: tactics for an officer, the industry for a manager. People differ too, but the white paper holds that there are general Leadership skills. Management has both: a general decision procedure and specific processes such as artillery fire control.
- **"As long as it does not restrict."** The same sentence is used for all three anchors. Doctrine, culture and processes should show *how* leaders can act, behave and proceed successfully, not *what* they must do. This is where [[Doctrine (Swiss Armed Forces)|doctrine]] enters the model.
- **The three aspects are not three jobs.** Every measure in one aspect has effects in the other two. Mission command is a Command approach, but it makes demands on Leadership (trust, tolerance of mistakes) and on Management (synchronising through a shared picture instead of detailed orders).
- **Nobody needs all three in full.** Leaders are part of teams whose members complement each other. In a flat team each member needs only a minimum in each aspect. In a hierarchy, skills are tied to functions.
  - A **commander** must have Command skills, and weak Leadership is hard to compensate. Gaps in Management can be covered by the staff.
  - A **staff officer** needs strong Command and Management skills. Weak Leadership matters less, but it is still a problem in war.
- **Good leading is a quality of the group.** It follows that the quality of leadership at one level includes the quality of the levels below it.
- **The model has two more layers.** Education and training are projected onto the same three aspects (the *Standardmodell*). So the model can also describe a training programme, for example as "too blue": much procedure, little purpose and little about people.

### Why "sui generis"

The white paper holds that military leading is a kind of its own, for two reasons [@hofstetter2023thesen].

- **Hot state and cold state.** An army has two states. The *hot state* is war. The *cold state* is the normal situation, which is bureaucracy. The cold state has no purpose of its own: it only prepares for the hot one. People are shaped by the state they live in, so leading in the cold state should be kept as close as possible to the hot state.
- **The mission can cost lives.** The Service Regulations define leading as bringing each individual to commit all their strength to the common mission, "im Ernstfall auch unter Einsatz des Lebens" [@dra1994dienstreglement, Art. 9]. The same regulations make the superior responsible for the welfare of subordinates. The model does not resolve this tension. It says the tension has to be endured, and that this is the reason to orient oneself towards people, not an exception to it.

## Origins & Evolution

1. **Mission versus people (1990s).** Rudolf Steiger, later commander of the Military Academy, brought the term *menschenorientierte Führung* into the Swiss debate. A public argument followed about mission-centred against people-oriented leading [@steiger1996auftragszentrierte]. Today the two are seen as a necessary tension, not a contradiction.
2. **Leadership versus management (1977).** In the civilian debate, Zaleznik asked whether managers and leaders are different kinds of people [@zaleznik1977managers]. The pair became a commonplace of management writing.
3. **Bungay's third term (2011).** The British historian and consultant Stephen Bungay argued that the pair leaves something out and proposed a trinity: management, leadership and **command**, the intellectual task of setting direction [@bungay2011executive]. For civilian use he also calls it *direction* [@bungay2011art].
4. **The British triangle (2021).** The British Army's leadership doctrine uses the same three words. The Swiss triangle borrows from it but defines the terms differently [@ukarmy2021leadership; @hofstetter2025mehrwert].
5. **The 95 theses (2023).** The Military Academy at ETH Zurich and the army's leadership training command wrote the model down as a paper of 95 theses, published in the army's journal *stratos* [@hofstetter2023thesen]. The Chief of the Armed Forces laid down that leading in the Swiss Army is to be understood in three parts.
6. **Adoption (2024–25).** On 27 March 2024 the army's leadership made the model the basis of all leadership training, for militia, professional military and civilian staff. In January 2025 it became part of the strategy for the army's *Vision 2030* [@hofstetter2025mehrwert].

Hofstetter stresses that the model is **not a new understanding of leadership**. It joins two existing pairs, the civilian "leadership versus management" and the military "mission versus people", into three dimensions and sharpens the terms [@hofstetter2025mehrwert].

## Application

- **Diagnosis.** When something goes wrong, ask which aspect failed. A wrong decision taken by a respected officer in a clean planning process is a Command failure. A sound plan that nobody follows is a Leadership failure. A sound plan, a willing unit and no ammunition at the right place is a Management failure.
- **Selection and staffing.** Describe a candidate's strengths by colour, then build the team so that the gaps are covered. A commander with weak Management needs a strong staff.
- **Training design.** Check that a course conveys purpose, values and order, and builds sureness in action, behaviour and procedure. Most courses lean towards one colour.
- **A worked example from the literature.** An infantry battalion has a reconnaissance platoon of about 20 soldiers. The mission requires exposing them. A commander who orders it without regard for the people entrusted to him violates his responsibility. A commander who holds them back out of false consideration endangers the other 830, and the platoon as well. The tension cannot be removed, only carried [@hofstetter2025mehrwert].
- **The course textbook.** The book *Auftrag. Mensch. Organisation.* is named after the three orientations and tells the model through the story of a militia officer [@hofstetter2026auftrag]. I have not worked through it yet.

### Not to be confused: Grint's problems

Keith Grint uses the same three words for a different idea. He sorts **problems**, not aspects [@grint2005problems]:

| Problem | Features | Fitting response |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | a crisis: little time, little doubt about what to do | **Command**: give the answer |
| **Tame** | complicated but known: it has been solved before | **Management**: apply the process |
| **Wicked** | complex, no clear solution, no stopping point | **Leadership**: ask questions, get people to work on it together |

In Grint's typology a situation calls for *one* of the three. In the Swiss model every situation has *all three* aspects at once. Grint also adds a warning the Swiss model does not have: leaders tend to *describe* a problem as the type that suits their preferred response. Someone who likes to command will see crises everywhere.

### Not to be confused: power, management and leadership in the research literature

The leadership literature of the course draws a different line. It separates leadership from **power** and from **management** [@day2012leadership; @bright2019principles]:

| | **Power** | **Management** | **Leadership** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | The capacity and the resources to influence | Coordination through formal structures and processes | Social influence towards collective purposes |
| Rests on | Bases of power: identification, expertise, reward and punishment, a legitimate role | Planning, organising, directing, controlling | The acceptance of the followers |
| Tied to a role? | Leading requires power | Usually tied to an appointed role | Appointed or emergent |

These are ideal types: in practice one person usually does all three.

- **Where Command fits.** The lecture places the CLM term in this scheme: command is the formal authority and responsibility of an appointed role. It rests mainly on **legitimate power**, which is one base of power among several. In practice a single order can work through authority, expertise, identification and norms at once.
- **"Leadership" is wider here.** In this literature leadership is an influence process between a leader and followers, explained by the leader's dispositions and behaviour, the followers' perceptions and attributions, and the context. That covers more than the Leadership aspect of CLM.
- **Two needs of a group.** The course reading says effective leadership serves **task needs** (progress towards the goal) and **maintenance needs** (members fit and work together). The Ohio State studies found the same split in leader behaviour: **initiating structure** and **consideration**. In CLM terms, task needs point to Command and Management, maintenance needs to Leadership. That mapping is mine.
- **Classical management is the ancestor of the Management aspect.**
    - **Fayol** described management as five functions (planning, organising, staffing, directing, controlling). Several of his principles come from military practice: unity of command, the scalar chain, discipline, *esprit de corps*, initiative.
    - **Weber** described the bureaucracy: specialised roles, a clear hierarchy, promotion by merit, strict rules, and impersonal authority that belongs to the office [@weber1922wirtschaft]. This is the white paper's **cold state**. Merton named its downside: rules become ends in themselves and block adaptation to situations nobody foresaw.
    - **Taylor** put the system before the person and separated planning from doing [@taylor1911principles]. See [[Mission Command#The opposite pole: Taylor]].
    - What the three share: rational systems coordinate and control, and the person of the leader matters less than the design of the organisation. CLM's claim that Management alone is not enough is aimed at exactly this view.

> [!counter] Critiques & Limits
> - **A working basis, by its own account.** The white paper says that the borders between the three aspects are still to be settled by research. The table is called a working basis (*Arbeitsgrundlage*). The model is young and I know of no empirical test of it.
> - **Fuzzy borders.** Is the planning process Management or Command? The white paper files the [[OODA Loop]] and sense-making under Management, though orientation looks like the "thinking about the task" that defines Command. In practice the three aspects cannot be observed separately.
> - **It sorts, it does not explain.** CLM is a taxonomy. It says which questions to ask, not how the aspects affect each other or how much of each a situation needs. [[General Model Theory]] applies: it is a reduction made for a purpose, here training and a common language.
> - **Three words, several meanings.** The British Army, Bungay, Grint and the Swiss Army each define command, leadership and management differently, and the research literature uses "leadership" for the whole influence process. In an answer, always say whose definition is meant.
> - **Symmetry may be too neat.** Every aspect gets one anchor, one educational aim and one training aim. Real organisations are less tidy, and the neat table may hide that Command carries the legal responsibility while the other two do not.

## Self-Test: CLM

> [!qard]- 1. Name the three aspects of leading in the Swiss model and what each is oriented towards.
> Command: the mission. Leadership: the people. Management: the organisation. Successful leading is mission-centred, people-oriented and organisation-based.

> [!qard]- 2. Which kind of challenge and which kind of solution belong to each aspect?
> Command: challenges of substance, conceptual solutions. Leadership: human challenges, communal solutions. Management: organisational challenges, institutional solutions.

> [!qard]- 3. Where is each aspect anchored in the organisation?
> Command in doctrine, Leadership in culture, Management in structures and processes. Each should support without restricting: show *how*, not prescribe *what*.

> [!qard]- 4. What do education and training contribute to each aspect?
> Education conveys purpose (Command), values (Leadership) and order (Management). Training builds sureness in action, in behaviour and in procedure.

> [!qard]- 5. Why is "leadership versus management" incomplete?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> A superior can meet every human and organisational demand and still fail, because the decision was wrong. Without expertise in the task, clean planning by a model officer still leads to wrong decisions. Command names that missing part.

> [!qard]- 6. Which skills does a commander need most, and which a staff officer?
> A commander needs Command; weak Leadership is hard to compensate, weak Management can be covered by the staff. A staff officer needs Command and Management; weak Leadership matters less, but is a problem in war.

> [!qard]- 7. What are the hot state and the cold state, and what follows for leading?
> Hot state: war. Cold state: the normal situation, a bureaucracy that exists only to prepare for war. People are shaped by the state they live in, so leading in the cold state should stay as close as possible to the hot state.

> [!qard]- 8. Apply: a well-liked company commander plans an attack by the book. The plan is tactically unsound and fails. Diagnose it with CLM.
> <!-- qard-write -->
> Leadership (the people follow) and Management (the procedure was clean) were in order. The failure is in Command: the substance of the decision. More team-building or a better planning process would not have helped.

> (1) Auftrag: the orientation. (2) Command: the aspect of leading. (3) Sinn: what education conveys. (4) Handlungssicherheit: what training builds. (5) Doktrin: where it is established.

> [!qard]- 10. Compare: how does Grint use command, management and leadership, and how does that differ from the Swiss model?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> Grint sorts problems: critical ones need command, tame ones management, wicked ones leadership. One response per problem. In the Swiss model the three are aspects of every act of leading, present at the same time.

> [!qard]- 11. Limit: name two weaknesses of the CLM model.
> The borders between the aspects are unsettled (is planning Command or Management?), and it is a taxonomy: it sorts questions but does not explain how the aspects interact or how much of each a situation needs.

> [!qard]- 12. Compare: how does the research literature separate power, management and leadership, and where does Command fit?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> Power is the capacity to influence, management is coordination through formal structures, leadership is social influence that rests on the followers' acceptance. Command is the formal authority of an appointed role and rests mainly on legitimate power, one base among several.

> [!qard]- 13. Name Weber's three types of legitimate authority.
> Traditional (it has always been so), charismatic (the person), legal-rational (rules and office). Bureaucracy is the organisation of the third.
