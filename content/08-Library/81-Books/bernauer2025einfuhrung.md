> [!bluf]
> The course textbook for Foundations of Political Science: a German-language introduction in 13 chapters (6th edition, Nomos). It is built around **one diagram of the political system** (figure 0.1) and works through its parts: citizens, intermediaries, and the central decision-making institutions, with international relations at the end.
>
> Its approach is **empirical-analytical**: define a concept, state a hypothesis, measure, test. Use it as the reference for definitions in the exam. Where a vault note says something the book does not, the note marks it.

## Summary

The book has three parts.

1. **Foundations** (ch. 1 and 2): what politics and political science are, the state as a political system, and how research works (question, theory, hypothesis, concept, measurement, test).
2. **National political systems** (ch. 3 to 12): first the basic forms (democracy and autocracy) and the systems of government, then the building blocks of figure 0.1 one by one. Germany, Austria, Switzerland and the EU are the running examples.
3. **Across borders** (ch. 13): international cooperation, war, globalisation, European integration.

Chapters 3 to 13 can be read in any order. Each ends with reading tips. The book has an online glossary and test questions.

## Key Claims

- (p. 8) **Figure 0.1** is the frame of the whole book: inputs, four process functions (interest articulation, interest aggregation, policy-making, implementation), outputs and feedback, with the state among other states. It is based on Powell et al. 2015.
- (p. 35) The sovereign territorial state consists of a **people, a territory and state authority** (three-element doctrine).
- (p. 117 to 118) Every political system has the same building blocks: **citizens, intermediaries, and a central decision-making system** of legislature, executive and judiciary.
- (p. 119 to 122) Democracy is rule by the people. Its most important mark is **free and fair elections**. Definitions run from **narrow** (elections, competition, changes of power) to **broad** (plus liberties, rule of law, separation of powers).
- (p. 127) An autocracy is a system that does not meet the criteria of democracy. The three types are **monarchies, military regimes and civilian regimes**, with two further dimensions: personalisation and restriction of freedom.
- (p. 133 to 138) There are several good measures of democracy and no perfect one. The choice must fit the definition that the argument needs.
- (p. 139 to 146) Democracies provide **more public goods** than autocracies. Two explanations: the median voter and the selectorate theory.
- (p. 156 to 158) Economic development does not cause democratisation, but it **stabilises** young democracies.
- (p. 163 to 165) Democracies limit state power by free and fair elections, the rule of law and the **separation of powers**, horizontal and vertical.
- (p. 181 to 182) Switzerland fits the typology of systems of government only to a limited extent.

## Evaluation

- **Reliable and current.** A standard textbook in its sixth edition, written by four professors of political science, with literature up to 2025.
- **One school.** The book stands in the empirical-analytical tradition and uses rational-choice arguments often (median voter, selectorate, veto players). Normative political theory and interpretive approaches get little room.
- **Regional focus.** The examples come mostly from Germany, Austria, Switzerland and the EU. That suits the course and limits the view on other regions.
- **Thin on security.** War and armed forces appear mainly in chapter 13. The security reading in the vault notes is my own.
- **Terms differ from other literature.** The book says "civilian regime" where others split into single-party and personalist regimes, and it does not use "directorial system" for Switzerland.
