> [!bluf]
> A one-volume survey of Britain and Ireland from 1780 to 1914 in 44 short chapters, written for students. It stands on the syllabus list of general surveys for History I. I use it as the **companion to the lecture**: for each session it gives the facts, the dates and usually the state of the historians' debate.
>
> It serves sessions 2, 3, 5, 7, 8 and 9 well. It has little on police and prisons (session 6), science (session 10) and immigration (session 11), and it stops in 1914, while the course runs to the 1930s.
>
> So far I have worked through the passages listed under *Key Claims*, not the whole book.

## Summary

The book is built in six sections that alternate between society and politics.

1. **Early industrial Britain, c. 1780–1850** (ch. 1–8): population, aristocracy and middle classes, the industrial revolution, towns, agriculture, and the conflicts that industry produced.
2. **Britain at war and peace, 1780–1815** (ch. 9–15): the American war, the impact of the French Revolution, the wars against France, Ireland and the Union.
3. **A new political era, 1815–1846** (ch. 16–22): radicalism and repression, the road to the Reform Act of 1832, Chartism, Peel.
4. **A mature industrial society, c. 1850–1914** (ch. 23–29): the economy, class, identity and gender, free trade and state regulation, the poor, education and leisure.
5. **Party, policy and diplomacy, 1846–1880** (ch. 30–35): the reform acts, Gladstone, Disraeli, foreign policy.
6. **Empire, democracy and the road to war, 1880–1914** (ch. 36–44): the Empire as a cultural icon, the parties, votes for women, Ireland, Labour and welfare, the origins of the First World War.

Each chapter is about ten pages. Boxes in the text define terms (laissez-faire, evangelicalism, Poor Law settlement) and give short lives of people (Burke, Chadwick, Booth, Rowntree). The lists of boxes at the front work as a glossary.

## Key Claims

**Industrial revolution (session 3)**

- (p. 42–43) Between 1780 and 1850 Britain went through the most radical economic and social change in its history: factories, mechanisation, canals and railways, coal and iron.
- (p. 44–46) Why Britain first: geography and cheap coal, a productive agriculture and fast urbanisation, landowners who invested in trade and mines, and a "fiscal-military state" under the rule of law that funded wars it mostly won.
- (p. 46–48) The debate: the older view saw a "take-off" in the 1780s. Later figures show gradual growth, with modern economic growth fully established only in the railway age from the 1830s. In 1860 only about 30% of jobs were in sectors transformed since 1780.
- (p. 48–50) The defence of "revolution": the data are fragile, change was concentrated in regions, and contemporaries were sure they lived through profound change. Even the revisionists keep the term.
- (p. 75) The Luddites of 1811–12 were skilled workers defending their craft, backed by their communities. They were not vandals.

**Ideologies (session 2)**

- (p. 80) Burke held that established traditions carry practical wisdom, and distrusted the abstract principles of the Enlightenment.
- (p. 84, 108) Paine's *Rights of Man* (1791–92) gave middle-class radicals and artisans the argument for parliamentary reform.
- (p. 214) The Reform Act of 1832 was made by Whig landowners to bring the lower middle classes inside the constitution, not to satisfy the radicals.
- (p. 267) Free trade was Britain's dominant economic ideology by the early 1860s. How it got there is fiercely debated.
- (p. 270–272) Laissez-faire meant a state reduced to defence and the legal frame. In practice governments regulated as a last resort, first for children (Factory Act 1833), who were not free agents.

**Towns, poverty and welfare (session 5)**

- (p. 51) The urban share of England's population: 23% in 1750, 34% in 1801, 54% in 1851, 78% in 1901.
- (p. 19) Early industrial towns were unhealthy. In the 1820s and 1830s death rates were often higher than the very high birth rates.
- (p. 287–288) The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 aimed at a uniform system and lower costs. Relief was to be less attractive than work, and the workhouse deterred for the rest of the century.
- (p. 291–292) Booth (London) and Rowntree (York, 1901) each found about 30% of the population in poverty, mostly because of low or irregular earnings. That supported state action.
- (p. 430) Old age pensions (1908) were paid from taxes. National insurance shared the cost between worker, employer and state.

**Empire, gender, class (sessions 7, 8, 9)**

- (p. 371–378) After the Mutiny India became a fully integrated colony. From 1876 ("Empress of India") the monarchy, the jubilees and the music halls sold the Empire to the public at home.
- (p. 264–265) A married woman's property passed to her husband and divorce was far easier for men. The "separate spheres" of public men and private women describe an ideal: the reality was more complex.
- (p. 404, 407–408) The campaign against the Contagious Diseases Act attacked a sexual double standard. The WSPU (1903) broke away from the constitutional suffragists and turned militant from 1905.
- (p. 248) Historians are now more cautious about claiming that industry produced classes hostile to each other. Status, where people saw themselves in a hierarchy, explains more than position in production.
- (p. 304–306) Leisure was defined and protected by law (bank holidays, 1871). Football and the music halls were shared by middle and working classes more than is often assumed.
- (p. 384) The Third Reform Act of 1884 gave the vote to about 60% of adult men.

## Evaluation

- **Reliable for facts and dates.** A standard student survey by a specialist on the period, with notes and a bibliography per chapter.
- **Strong on debates where it has a chapter.** Chapter titles are often questions ("Industrial revolution or industrial evolution?", "An accidental catastrophe?"), which fits a course about contested topics.
- **Weighted towards politics.** About half the chapters are on parties, governments and diplomacy, which the lecture only touches on.
- **Gaps against the syllabus.** Police and prisons, science, sexuality and immigration get single pages at best, and the book ends in 1914.
- **A survey, not a source.** For the source interpretation and for the debates, the required reading of each session comes first. Evans is where to check a date or get the overview.
