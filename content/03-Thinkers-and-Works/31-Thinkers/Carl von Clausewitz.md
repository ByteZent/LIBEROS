> [!bluf]
> Prussian officer and theorist (1780–1831) whose unfinished [[On War]] is still the foundational text of Western strategic theory. He is indispensable for one idea above all: **war is an instrument of policy**, and its conduct must stay subordinate to political purpose, even though war's own nature of violence, chance and friction constantly pulls against that control.

## Context

- Served in the Prussian army from age 12. Fought and was captured in the 1806 defeat at Jena–Auerstedt, a formative experience of a state collapsing militarily.
- Part of the Prussian reform circle around Scharnhorst and Gneisenau.
- Entered Russian service in 1812 rather than serve alongside Napoleon, and returned to Prussian service for the 1813–15 campaigns.
- Director of the War College (*Allgemeine Kriegsschule*) in Berlin from 1818 to 1830, where he wrote most of *Vom Kriege*.
- Died of cholera in 1831. His widow, Marie von Clausewitz, published his works in 1832–34.

## Core Ideas

- **War as continuation of policy.** War is "a continuation of political intercourse, with the addition of other means" (Book I, ch. 1, §24). See [[On War]].
- **The paradoxical trinity.** War is shaped by (1) primordial violence, hatred and enmity; (2) the play of chance and probability; (3) its subordination as an instrument of policy. These are commonly (and loosely) mapped onto people, army and government.
- **Friction.** "Everything in war is very simple, but the simplest thing is difficult." Friction is what separates real war from war on paper.
- **[[Center of Gravity]]** (*Schwerpunkt*): "the hub of all power and movement, on which everything depends."
- **Culminating point.** Attacks and victories exhaust themselves; beyond a certain point, continuing weakens the attacker.
- **Absolute vs real war.** Pure logic drives war towards extremes; political purpose, friction and uncertainty moderate it in reality.

## Key Works

- [[On War]] (*Vom Kriege*, 1832)

## Influence

- Moltke the Elder and the Prussian/German general staff tradition, though often read selectively.
- Rediscovered in the US after Vietnam through the Howard–Paret translation (1976). It shaped the post-Vietnam reform of US Army doctrine and the Weinberger–Powell criteria.
- Concepts such as [[Center of Gravity]] and culmination are embedded in NATO and US joint doctrine.

> [!counter] Critiques
> - **Liddell Hart** blamed a misread "Mahdi of mass" Clausewitz for the slaughter of 1914–18 (disputed: the misreading was not Clausewitz's).
> - **John Keegan** and **Martin van Creveld** argued his state-centric framework doesn't fit pre-state, ethnic or "non-trinitarian" war.
> - Defenders (e.g. Christopher Bassford, Antulio Echevarria) reply that the trinity describes *forces*, not institutions, so it applies to any war.
