> [!bluf]
> The Tory governments of the 1820s were divided by two ways of thinking about the state. **Liberal Tories** (Canning, Huskisson, Peel) saw society as a machine that runs by natural laws: the state should set fixed rules, apply them neutrally and stay out. **High Tories** (Eldon, Sidmouth, Vansittart, Castlereagh) saw society as an organism that needs constant management: the state should use discretion, influence and paternal care.
>
> The line is not "commerce against land" and not "mild against harsh". Liberal Tories were often the harsher ones, and high Tories the more generous to the poor. The difference is **rule against discretion**.
>
> The "liberalism" in liberal Toryism was not optimistic. It rested on evangelical religion and on Malthus: the market is a moral order that rewards virtue and punishes vice, and suffering teaches.

## Question

What divided the ministers of Lord Liverpool's governments, if most of them agreed on most policies? And what does the division show about the meaning of "liberal" before 1850?

It matters for the course because this is where "the rise of conservatism and liberalism" starts: liberal Toryism passes through Peel into Gladstone's liberalism [@hilton2006mad, p. 315]. Hilton himself says it would be less misleading to call it liberal Conservatism [@hilton2006mad, p. 308].

## Argument

### What liberal Toryism was not

1. **Not a turn from agriculture to industry.** Liberal Tories did not set out to promote trade at the expense of land. Freer markets and sound money were meant to return the economy to a *natural* state, which many expected to shrink output and help agriculture. Most bankers, merchants and manufacturers opposed cash payments in 1819, and most City businessmen backed the high Tories [@hilton2006mad, p. 310].
2. **Not a bid for public opinion** to avoid constitutional reform. There was no such thing as one public opinion between 1792 and 1848. Politicians meant the "respectable" part that agreed with them [@hilton2006mad, p. 311].
3. **Not humanity.** Peel as Home Secretary was for "salutary terror" [@hilton2006mad, p. 318].

### Two languages

John Stuart Mill wrote in 1840 that every Englishman is either a Benthamite or a Coleridgian. For the first the world is a contrived machine, for the second a web or organism that no mortal can fully understand and that a paternal elite must guide. Hilton applies this to the Tories: *politically* the liberal Tories were closer to Coleridge, but in their mode of thinking they were the Benthamites and the high Tories the Coleridgians [@hilton2006mad, p. 313–314]. A mechanist could slip into organic language. The reverse was unthinkable: no high Tory would have spoken, like Peel, of "the machine of government" [@hilton2006mad, p. 315].

### The comparison

| | Liberal Tories | High Tories |
|---|---|---|
| **People** | Canning, Huskisson, Peel, Robinson. Intellectual: Copleston. Spiritual guide: Chalmers | Eldon, Sidmouth, Vansittart (Bexley), Castlereagh, Rose. In print: Southey |
| **Picture of society** | A machine with natural laws and self-correction | An organism that needs tending |
| **Task of the state** | Operate neutrally, according to rule. Small but visible | Manage, interfere and use discretion, case by case, through contacts and officials |
| **Fear** | "The encroachments of power, and the errors of empiricism" | A state without the means to act and protect |
| **Money** | Convertibility into gold: a "natural" currency out of the hands of ministers and bankers (1819) | A "discretionary" paper currency. Finance managed hand in glove with Rothschild and Baring |
| **Criminal law** | Fixed, graded, certain penalties. Fewer capital offences, strictly enforced | Severe laws that are rarely enforced. Wide discretion for judges, JPs and victims |
| **Order** | A uniformed, preventive police (1829). No spies | Secret services, informers, the "arbitrary discretion of magistrates" |
| **The poor** | Moral paternalism (church-building, vagrancy laws) but no material welfare: deterrence and self-help. For the New Poor Law of 1834 | Material paternalism: friendly societies, savings banks, outdoor relief, a minimum wage. Against the New Poor Law, more open to factory laws |
| **Business in distress** | No rescue: no exchequer bills in 1826 | Help and bargains with the private sector |
| **Foreign policy** | Canning: each nation states its intentions, the balance of power is a natural mechanism. "Each nation for itself and God for us all" | Castlereagh: congresses, personal negotiation, great powers arranging the small ones |
| **Where truth lies** | In a timeless, mechanical present | In the past |

Sources for the table: [@hilton2006mad, p. 315–325, 340, 350].

### Why rule against discretion is the real line

Hilton's clearest case is the criminal law. On a single scale from harsh to lenient the positions make no sense. On two axes they do [@hilton2006mad, p. 320]:

| | Discretionary justice | Fixed penalties |
|---|---|---|
| **Harsh** | Ellenborough | Peel |
| **Lenient** | Eldon, Mackintosh | Bentham |

Peel and Bentham stand in the same column and Peel and Eldon, both Tories, in different ones. Peel let more people hang in the 1820s than any predecessor, and Eldon, the "reactionary", was the squeamish one [@hilton2006mad, p. 318].

### The morality behind the market

Liberal Tories needed a neutral system for a moral reason. If paupers are to face the workhouse and failed businessmen prison for debt, they must not be able to blame events outside their control. Under a managed currency they could. Under a natural one the market becomes an arena in which God speaks directly to people. Peel called it a natural "system of social retribution", with "just reward of merit, and just penalty of folly and vice" [@hilton2006mad, p. 325–326].

This is why they were not optimists. They were Malthusians who believed the world's resources to be finite, looked for social justice more than for growth, and blamed distress on speculation and excess. Any interference by the ruling class, caring or coercive, would disturb the machine [@hilton2006mad, p. 326–327]. Compare [[Utilitarianism]]: the same mechanism, but Bentham expected happiness from it.

### What became of it

The same policies (free trade, sound money, retrenchment) became public doctrine after 1850, but in a different spirit: as "Cobdenism", a recipe for growth and peace [@hilton2006mad, p. 326]. The ideology passed through Peelism into Gladstonian liberalism [@hilton2006mad, p. 315].

## Counter-Arguments

> [!counter] Emphasis and style, not substance
> Many historians hold that the antagonisms among Liverpool's ministers were of emphasis and style only. Hilton grants much of this himself [@hilton2006mad, p. 309, 314–315]:
>
> - Most differences were of emphasis. Wellington agreed that Huskisson was "in principle" right on commerce.
> - Both groups resisted parliamentary reform, and both claimed Pitt's inheritance.
> - The issue that divided ministers most, the Catholic question, cut across the line: Peel opposed emancipation, Castlereagh supported it.
> - Both favoured local authority (magistrates, employers, Poor Law guardians) and accepted the same core tasks of central government.
> - It is hard to say to which camp Liverpool, the Prime Minister, belonged.
> - "Liberal Toryism" is a label of historians. Hilton uses it out of deference to convention.
>
> His answer: a "still-life" of 1825 would hardly show the distinction, but it is worth making because of what followed. Two elements of Pitt's legacy, political authoritarianism and economic libertarianism, grew less and less compatible during the 1820s.

> [!counter] Not every evangelical was a liberal Tory
> Liberal Toryism was fired by an evangelical ethos, but welfare paternalists such as Shaftesbury were evangelicals too. Hilton places them, with their expectation of an imminent Apocalypse and their rejection of natural law, closer to Coleridge and the high Tories [@hilton2006mad, p. 342].

## Conclusion

The division is real but it is one of mentality, not of party, interest or kindness. Its use for the course:

- **"Liberal" does not mean generous or progressive here.** Before 1850 the liberal position is the minimal, rule-bound, morally severe state. Southey could still use "liberal" in the old sense of generous public spending [@hilton2006mad, p. 321].
- **Paternalism was the conservative alternative**, not a later invention of the left. The interventionist state has a high Tory ancestry.
- **The same policy can carry opposite meanings.** Free trade under Peel is a discipline. Under Cobden it is a promise.

## Self-Test: Liberal vs High Toryism

> [!qard]- 1. Recall: what is the basic difference between liberal and high Tories?
> <!-- qard-important -->
> Rule against discretion. Liberal Tories want a small, neutral state that applies fixed rules to a self-regulating society. High Tories want a state that manages by discretion, influence and paternal care.

> [!qard]- 2. Recall: name three liberal Tories and three high Tories.
> Liberal: Canning, Huskisson, Peel. High: Eldon, Sidmouth, Vansittart (also Castlereagh, Rose).

> [!qard]- 3. Explain: why is "commerce against land" the wrong way to read the division?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> Liberal Tories wanted a natural economy, not more industry, and most supported the Corn Law of 1815. Most bankers and manufacturers opposed cash payments in 1819 and backed the high Tories.

> [!qard]- 4. Explain: which issue does Hilton call the defining one, and why?
> The resumption of cash payments in 1819. Gold convertibility replaced a discretionary currency by a natural one. Nearly all high Tories resisted it, liberals of both parties supported it.

> [!qard]- 5. Apply: place Peel, Eldon, Bentham and Ellenborough on the criminal law.
> <!-- qard-write -->
> Two axes. Peel: harsh and fixed. Bentham: lenient and fixed. Ellenborough: harsh and discretionary. Eldon: soft and discretionary. So Peel shares a column with Bentham, not with Eldon.

> [!qard]- 6. Apply: who supported and who opposed the New Poor Law of 1834 among the Tories, and why?
> Liberal Tories such as Peel supported it: deterrence and self-help, no material welfare. High Tories (Sidmouth, Eldon, Vansittart) opposed it as hazardous: the poor are owed paternal care.

> [!qard]- 7. Explain: why did liberal Tories need a *neutral* monetary system for moral reasons?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> If paupers and bankrupts are to be punished, they must not be able to blame a capricious, managed system. In a natural market, success and failure show merit and vice: a "system of social retribution".

> [!qard]- 8. Compare: how does liberal Toryism differ from the Cobdenism that followed after 1850?
> Same policies (free trade, sound money, retrenchment), different spirit. Liberal Tories were Malthusian pessimists who sought justice. Cobdenism promised growth, rising wages and peace.

> [!qard]- 9. Limit: what speaks against a sharp distinction between the two groups?
> <!-- qard-write -->
> Differences were often of emphasis. Both resisted parliamentary reform and claimed Pitt. The Catholic question cut across the line (Peel against, Castlereagh for). The label comes from historians.
